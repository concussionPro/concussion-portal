## POSITIONING SPINE — a named practitioner situation, not a general concussion pitch

> Internal framing note. The argument below governs every submission section. It is not a general concussion-awareness pitch, and it must not be allowed to drift into one.

**The shift.** Amsterdam 2022 moved concussion from a **rest** condition to an **exercise-prescription** condition, gated on exercise testing and on an individually derived heart-rate threshold. Prescribed individualised exercise shortened median recovery from 17 days to 13 days in the pivotal randomised trial (Leddy et al., *JAMA Pediatrics* 2019; n=103 adolescent athletes aged 13-18, P=.009), and reduces the incidence of persisting symptoms.

**Why that leaves a gap.** Every relevant profession was trained **before** that shift. A survey of **1,272 physiotherapists** found **70% already held concussion training** and averaged **13 out of 15** on concussion knowledge — with the specific gaps in return-to-play confidence and in knowing **when graded exertional training is indicated**. **The line to land: they are not ignorant — they are current on the old model.** CRM is the update to the new model. Never frame it as remedial education or as fixing ignorance; CASES members are accredited scientists and will read that framing as condescension.

**The specific practitioner situation this course is built for — and this is the spine of the CASES case.** The demand here is real but narrow, and it is best stated as a named situation rather than a general need:

> In performance settings, **the person actually running graded exertion after a concussion is very often the sport and exercise scientist** — the physiology support staff member in an academy, a professional club, a university programme or an NGB pathway. They are the one on the treadmill or the bike with the athlete, holding the heart-rate monitor, deciding whether today's session progresses or regresses. The graded-exertion component of the return-to-play pathway is delegated to them in practice.
>
> They hold the underlying competency — graded exercise testing, heart-rate-based prescription, symptom and load monitoring, objective progression — because that is what their degree and accreditation are built on. What they have **no formal training in** is the concussion-specific application of it: the standardised BCTT/BCBT protocol and its termination criteria, what the symptom-limited heart-rate threshold physiologically represents, the 80–90% prescription band derived from it, the re-test-driven progression rule, and the scope boundary that separates their part of the pathway from the medical acts of diagnosis and return-to-contact clearance.

That mismatch — carrying the graded-exertion responsibility in practice without formal training in the concussion protocol governing it — is the demand. It is narrow, it is specific, it is a duty-of-care exposure for the practitioner and the employing organisation, and it is exactly what an 8-credit CPD programme can close.

**Corollaries for how the pack is written:** lead with the practitioner in the performance setting, not with concussion statistics. Keep scope discipline prominent — it is what makes the argument credible rather than a scope grab. Do not claim UK practitioners are unaware of concussion; claim that the graded-exertion protocol they are already running is not something they were formally trained to run.

# APPLICATION CONTENT

## 1. Provider

Concussion Education Australia (CEA) is a specialist provider of concussion education for health, sport and exercise professionals. CEA publishes to depth in a single condition rather than breadth across many: every programme concerns the recognition, assessment, rehabilitation and return-to-activity management of concussion and mild traumatic brain injury. Programmes are authored in-house by a registered clinician, referenced to the primary peer-reviewed literature, and written to explicit scope-of-practice boundaries.

## 2. Course description

**Concussion Rehab Mastery** trains sport and exercise practitioners to deliver the exercise-based treatment of concussion that international consensus now places first-line.

For two decades, concussion management meant rest until symptoms cleared. The evidence overturned that. The 6th International Conference on Concussion in Sport (Amsterdam, October 2022; Patricios et al., *British Journal of Sports Medicine*, 57(11), 695–711) concluded that **sub-symptom-threshold aerobic exercise, prescribed from a heart-rate threshold derived by a graded exercise test, is first-line treatment**. The test that produces that threshold is the Buffalo Concussion Treadmill Test (BCTT), developed and validated over fifteen years by Leddy, Willer and colleagues at the University at Buffalo, whose randomised trials — in *JAMA Pediatrics* (2019) and *Lancet Child & Adolescent Health* (2021) — established that early prescribed aerobic exercise shortens recovery.

That is exercise-physiology work. Deriving an individualised heart-rate target from a symptom-limited graded exercise test, prescribing a dose against it, monitoring symptom response in real time, and progressing load against objective criteria is the core competence of the sport and exercise scientist.

**The practitioner situation the programme is built around.** In performance environments the graded-exertion component of the concussion pathway is, in practice, delegated to the sport and exercise scientist. The physiology support staff member in an academy, professional club, university programme or NGB pathway is the one administering the exertion test, holding the heart-rate data, and making the session-by-session progress-or-regress call. That responsibility sits with them because the underlying competency sits with them — but the concussion-specific training does not. Very few have had formal instruction in the standardised BCTT and BCBT protocols and their two distinct termination criteria, in what the symptom-limited heart-rate threshold represents physiologically, in the 80–90% prescription band derived from it, in the re-test-driven progression rule, or in where their lane ends relative to the medical acts of diagnosis and return-to-contact clearance.

That is the gap: not concussion awareness, which is generally good, but formal training in a protocol these practitioners are already running. What the programme supplies is the concussion-specific protocol, the phenotype reasoning, and — critically — a clear account of where the practitioner's lane ends.

The programme delivers all three across eight modules: the pathophysiology that explains why exercise works and why it must stay below threshold; recognition and red-flag triage; the complete BCTT and Buffalo Concussion Bike Test protocols; conversion of the heart-rate threshold into a full FITT prescription; phenotype-specific rehabilitation across the seven recognised concussion phenotypes; the six-stage graded return-to-sport strategy with load monitoring and reconditioning physiology; persistent post-concussion symptoms and the programme that will not progress; and the documentation and inter-professional communication that close the loop with the referring clinician.

**Scope discipline runs through every module.** Diagnosis is a medical act. Return-to-contact clearance is a medical act. The practitioner trained by this programme implements graded exertion, measures it, documents it, escalates red flags, and hands a prepared athlete back to the clinician who holds the clearance decision. Learners are assessed on that boundary as rigorously as on the protocol.

## 5. Delivery mode

Wholly online, asynchronous and self-paced, delivered on CEA's own learning platform. Individual learner authentication; per-module progress tracking; no cohort scheduling or live attendance, so UK practitioners can complete it around clinic, training and fixture commitments.

Each module combines structured written instruction, worked clinical calculation (heart-rate threshold conversion, target-band derivation, load progression), protocol walkthroughs, decision frameworks and copyable documentation artifacts. Progress is retained across sessions, and access continues after completion so the material serves as a working reference.

Certification on completion states the module titles, the assessed instructional hours and the credits earned. On endorsement, the CASES credits logo and endorsement reference would appear on the certificate and the product page.

## 6. Assessment

Assessment is **distributed and per-module**, not a single terminal exam.

- **87 assessment questions**, allocated across the eight modules in proportion to weight.
- Questions are delivered **within each module**, at the point of learning, so a learner cannot advance past a protocol step they have not understood.
- **80% pass mark** at module level.
- Items test protocol accuracy (per-minute BCTT increments, the two termination criteria), physiological reasoning (why the symptom threshold represents an autonomic and cerebrovascular ceiling), clinical decision-making (progress, regress, refer) and scope discipline, including red-flag escalation and the boundary at return-to-contact clearance.
- Every item returns a written rationale on answer, making an incorrect response a teaching event.
- Certification requires every module at or above the pass mark.

The primary audience is the practitioner described in Email 2 and section 2: the person in a performance or clinical setting who is already the one running graded exertion after concussion.

- **Sport and exercise scientists working in physiology support roles** in professional and academy sport, university sport, and national governing body programmes.

- **Exercise referral practitioners and advanced personal trainers** on the CIMSPA register who receive referrals from healthcare professionals.

**Prerequisite knowledge assumed:** undergraduate-level exercise physiology — cardiorespiratory response to exercise, heart-rate and RPE monitoring, graded exercise test principles, FITT programming. The programme does not teach these; it applies them to concussion.

## 8. Learning outcomes

The programme carries **51 assessed learning objectives**, reproduced in full below as delivered.

### Module 1 — Concussion for the Exercise Physiologist
- Define concussion as a functional brain injury, distinguish it from structural traumatic brain injury, and explain why standard neuroimaging is typically normal.
- Describe the biomechanical mechanisms of concussion — rotational, linear, coup-contrecoup and diffuse axonal injury — and explain why rotational forces are considered most damaging.
- Trace the neurometabolic cascade from mechanoporation through ionic flux, Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase overdrive, hyperglycolysis, Ca²⁺-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction and neuroinflammation to recovery — and connect each phase to clinical symptoms.
- Explain cerebral blood-flow dysregulation after concussion, including impaired autoregulation, neurovascular uncoupling and reduced cerebrovascular CO₂ reactivity, and describe how each contributes to symptom provocation during exertion.
- Describe post-concussion autonomic dysfunction — sympathovagal imbalance, blunted baroreflex sensitivity and reduced HRV — and explain the mechanism of exercise intolerance.
- Justify why sub-symptom-threshold aerobic exercise is now first-line care rather than prolonged rest, citing the evidence base that drove the paradigm shift.
- Locate the exercise practitioner clearly within the multidisciplinary concussion team as the implementer of graded exertion, not the diagnostician or the clearer.

### Module 2 — Recognition, Red Flags & Scope of Practice
- Identify the observable signs and reported symptom clusters of concussion, and explain the neurophysiological mechanism underlying each cluster.
- List the red flags that mandate urgent emergency escalation, and describe the pathophysiological mechanism — intracranial haemorrhage, second-impact syndrome and cervical spine injury — that makes each a medical emergency requiring immediate emergency services activation.
- Articulate the scope boundary in concussion with its medico-legal rationale: what the exercise practitioner can and cannot do, and why diagnosis and medical clearance require medical authority.
- Describe how SCAT6 and SCOAT6 function as serial outcome measures within scope, and identify the critical limits of these tools that prevent them from being used for diagnosis or clearance by a non-medical practitioner.
- Navigate referral-in and referral-out pathways: describe how a concussion case appropriately reaches the exercise practitioner, recognise when symptoms warrant routing to vestibular physiotherapy, a sports physician, psychology or neurology, and describe the practitioner's position as active-rehabilitation specialist in the multidisciplinary team.

### Module 3 — Assessment That Is the Treatment (BCTT/BCBT)
- Explain the exercise-physiology rationale for a graded exertion protocol in concussion — specifically why a near-linear cardiovascular ramp identifies the autonomic and cerebrovascular ceiling that characterises the physiological phenotype.
- Identify all pre-test contraindications and red flags, categorise them as absolute or relative, and describe the correct response to each.
- Administer the standardised Balke-based Buffalo Concussion Treadmill Test (BCTT), including the precise per-minute incline and speed increments, continuous heart rate/RPE/symptom monitoring, and the two distinct test-termination criteria.
- Record the heart rate at symptom threshold (HRt), describe what it represents physiologically (the point where impaired cerebral autoregulation and blunted cerebrovascular CO₂ reactivity can no longer meet exertional demand), and distinguish it from the voluntary-exhaustion endpoint.
- Select and administer the Buffalo Concussion Bike Test (BCBT) when the treadmill protocol is contraindicated or clinically suboptimal, and describe the validated protocol steps.
- Convert the HRt into an individualised sub-symptom-threshold aerobic prescription using the 80–90% rule, including frequency, duration and mode.
- Apply a structured re-testing schedule to progress the symptom-threshold ceiling, identify the physiological-recovery endpoint, and communicate findings to the referring clinician within scope.
- Use VOMS, BESS and timed tandem gait as serial adjunct outcome measures to track vestibular, ocular and postural-control recovery — distinguishing their role from diagnostic assessment.

### Module 4 — Sub-Symptom-Threshold Aerobic Rehabilitation
- Explain the full biochemical and physiological rationale for sub-symptom-threshold aerobic exercise — covering autonomic restoration, cerebral autoregulation and CO₂ reactivity normalisation, exercise-induced BDNF upregulation and neuroplasticity, neuroinflammation reduction, and systemic deconditioning reversal.
- Describe the heart-rate threshold (HRt) as the physiological marker of the autonomic/cerebrovascular ceiling, and explain why training at 80–90% of HRt (rather than at or above it) produces adaptive recovery.
- Derive a complete FITT prescription from a BCTT HRt result, including worked calculations of the target HR band.
- Apply the real-time symptom-monitoring rule (≥2-point rise = stop/reduce), distinguishing acceptable baseline noise from genuine threshold-crossing.
- Progress the programme safely using the re-test-driven progression algorithm — stepping the HR ceiling as tolerance recovers, and distinguishing the "duration first, then intensity" sequencing rule.
- Select appropriate modalities for the deconditioned, dizzy, anxious or otherwise complex patient, and adapt the prescription to the presentation.
- Recognise red flags that require regression, pause or urgent escalation, and communicate these to the referrer within scope.

### Module 5 — Phenotype-Specific Exercise Rehabilitation
- Describe the neurophysiological mechanism underlying each of the seven concussion phenotypes (vestibular, oculomotor, cervicogenic, autonomic/physiologic, cognitive/fatigue, affective, headache/migraine) and connect each mechanism to its clinical presentation.
- Prescribe, dose and progress the in-scope exercise rehabilitation for each phenotype — including VOR×1/VOR×2 gaze stabilisation, habituation and dynamic balance for the vestibular phenotype; convergence, saccade, pursuit and accommodative facility drills for the oculomotor phenotype; cranio-cervical flexion, joint-position-error retraining and postural endurance work for the cervicogenic phenotype; and sub-symptom-threshold aerobic exercise for the autonomic phenotype.
- Explain the cerebellar neuroplasticity mechanism by which VOR gaze-stabilisation exercises restore vestibular function, and distinguish central vestibular adaptation from peripheral otoconia repositioning.
- Describe the cervico-ocular reflex and explain how cervical proprioceptive injury contributes to dizziness and gaze instability after concussion.
- Apply symptom-titrated dosing and a three-phase progression framework across all phenotypes, including the criteria for advancing, regressing and referring.
- Identify the dominant phenotype driver when multiple phenotypes co-present, sequence the treatment plan accordingly, and layer secondary phenotype exercises appropriately.
- Recognise the presentations — BPPV, manifest tropia, clinical depression, vertebrobasilar red flags — that require referral before or instead of exercise, and identify the appropriate specialist for each.

### Module 6 — Graded Return to Activity, Sport & Performance
- Apply the international graded return-to-sport (GRTS) strategy as a staged, symptom-monitored progression — with particular depth on stages 3 and 4 and what distinguishes each from the preceding stage.
- Comply with mandated stand-down requirements and correctly apply both minimums (calendar stand-down and symptom-free period) to a real case, identifying which governs.
- Explain the physiology of reconditioning — why aerobic base must precede sport-specific load, and the specific mechanisms by which running, multidirectional movement and contact-preparation each introduce qualitatively new physiological demands.
- Design a concussion-specific return-to-running progression and team-sport conditioning programme, applying objective progress, regress and readiness criteria governed by the 24-hour symptom rule.
- Coordinate physical and cognitive load demands (return-to-learn and return-to-work pacing) and communicate an athlete's reconditioning readiness to the medical practitioner responsible for contact clearance.

### Module 7 — Persistent Symptoms & the Complex Case
- Define persistent post-concussion symptoms (PPCS) using the current consensus timeframe and terminology, and explain why PPCS is a heterogeneous clinical picture rather than a single diagnosis.
- Describe the five pathophysiological mechanisms that drive persistence beyond the acute phase: sustained autonomic and cerebrovascular dysregulation, unresolved neuroinflammation, central sensitisation, the deconditioning spiral, and psychological maintaining factors including fear-avoidance and catastrophising.
- Identify the clinical and demographic risk factors that predict prolonged recovery, and explain why early activity avoidance is itself a modifiable, iatrogenic risk factor.
- Justify, from the evidence base, why graded sub-symptom-threshold aerobic exercise remains indicated — and actively therapeutic — in PPCS when a genuine symptom-limited threshold is confirmed.
- Apply a four-step structured decision framework when a rehabilitation programme stalls: reassess the BCTT threshold, screen for non-aerobic drivers, re-refer with data, and escalate red flags immediately.
- Adapt rehabilitation for the comorbid, deconditioned and anxious patient using pacing, graded exposure and behaviour-change strategies within scope.
- Recognise presentations that require referral beyond the exercise-practitioner lane — including progressive presentations suggesting neurodegenerative concern — and describe what an accurate, in-scope response looks like in practice.

### Module 8 — Documentation, Communication & Referral
- Produce the four core in-scope concussion-rehabilitation documents: assessment/BCTT record, session and progression tracking sheet, practitioner-to-referrer communication letter, and return-to-activity progress note — each as a real, structured artifact with scope-appropriate language.
- Apply the record-within-scope principle: frame findings as exercise-physiology observations and recommendations, never as a diagnosis or medical clearance for return to contact.
- Explain the three jobs every clinical record performs simultaneously — clinical continuity, inter-professional communication, and medico-legal accountability — and recognise why all three apply to concussion records.
- Apply the privacy principles governing the health information collected, stored and disclosed in the course of concussion rehabilitation, including the obligations triggered by AI scribe tools.
- Use AI-assisted documentation tools as an efficiency aid while retaining full personal professional responsibility for the accuracy and scope-appropriateness of every clinical note filed.

## 9. Suitability for a UK setting

CASES requires that endorsed CPD suits a UK setting. CEA addresses that on three axes.

**a) The clinical protocol is international, not jurisdictional.** The BCTT, the heart-rate threshold, the 80–90% prescription band, the ≥2-point symptom-exacerbation stop rule, the re-test-driven progression algorithm, the seven-phenotype framework and the six-stage graded return-to-sport strategy all derive from the international consensus statement and the peer-reviewed literature behind it. They are applied identically in Manchester and in Melbourne. Nothing in the physiological content of Modules 1, 3, 4, 5 or 7 is Australia-specific.

**b) UK return-to-play governance is addressed directly.** Module 6 teaches the competency of applying a mandated stand-down correctly: identifying the two governing minimums (a calendar stand-down and a symptom-free period), determining which one governs in a given case, and refusing to progress an athlete past the stage the governance permits. For UK learners this competency is exercised against **the UK Government/DCMS *UK Concussion Guidelines for Non-Elite (Grassroots) Sport*** and, where the athlete is under a national governing body, that NGB's return-to-play protocol — including the graduated return-to-play frameworks operated by the RFU, the FA, the Football Association's and the Rugby Football League's concussion protocols, and World Rugby's graduated return-to-play. CEA supplies a **UK annex** setting the UK instruments alongside the module content and mapping the stand-down and clearance rules learners must apply. The Australian instrument cited in the core content is presented as the worked example, not as the governing rule for a UK practitioner.

**c) UK practitioner scope is addressed directly.** The scope boundary the programme teaches maps cleanly onto UK practice. In the UK, as in Australia, **diagnosis of concussion and medical clearance for return to contact are medical acts**. A CASES-accredited sport and exercise scientist, a UKSCA-accredited S&C coach, a CIMSPA exercise referral practitioner and an AHCS-registered clinical exercise physiologist all operate under a referral-in relationship with a medical practitioner and none of them clears an athlete for contact. Module 2 teaches recognise-and-escalate rather than diagnose; Module 8 teaches the practitioner to write findings as exercise-physiology observations and recommendations, never as diagnosis or clearance. Where an HCPC-registered physiotherapist takes the course, the boundary shifts only in that their own professional standards govern; the material states that the practitioner's own regulator and professional body define their lane, and directs learners to it.

**d) Record-keeping and data protection.** Module 8 teaches the substantive principles — lawful collection, use and disclosure limits, cross-border transfer obligations, security of health information, and the specific obligations triggered by AI scribe tools — and the delivered examples cite the Australian instrument. The UK annex sets out the corresponding **UK GDPR and Data Protection Act 2018** obligations, including special category health data, so a UK learner applies the identical competency under the instrument that binds them.

**e) Language, units and currency of content.** The programme is written in British/Australian English spelling, uses SI units throughout, and is referenced to journals — *British Journal of Sports Medicine*, *JAMA Pediatrics*, *Lancet Child & Adolescent Health*, *Clinical Journal of Sport Medicine* — that UK practitioners read.
