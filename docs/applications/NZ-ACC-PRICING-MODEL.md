# ⚠️ CORRECTION — 2026-07-20. READ THIS BEFORE ANYTHING BELOW.

**The percentage-of-their-revenue model in this document is withdrawn. Do not quote it.**

Two failures, both caught by the owner:

**1. The volumes are invented.** Active+ at "730 episodes / NZ$705,910 of exercise-related billing" is not researched. It was produced by taking a modelled national pool (~4,000 episodes — itself a 15–25% referral-rate assumption applied to ~21,000 claims) and dividing it across 25 suppliers weighted by size. **No supplier's actual concussion volume is known.** Presenting a price as "3.1% of their billing" gave invented inputs the appearance of arithmetic. A procurement lead who knows their own numbers would have found the error in the first meeting.

**2. NZ$22,000 is not a realistic price for this product.** It is a monitoring tool that produces a report, sold by a vendor with no New Zealand accreditation, no outcome data and no reference customer. Percentage-of-customer-revenue pricing requires (a) that you can verify the revenue and (b) that you can demonstrate you caused it. Neither is true here.

## What replaces it

**NZ$49 per actively-delivering clinician, per month.** A six-clinician team is NZ$3,528/yr; a twenty-clinician service is NZ$11,760/yr.

Priced against **what comparable clinical software costs per clinician** — the practice-management and rehab-prescription tools these clinicians already pay for — because that is the comparison a practice manager actually makes, and it is checkable. It is not priced against ACC billing.

Counted on clinicians **delivering the service**, not organisational headcount. This matters at scale: Active+ has 1,400+ clinicians but only a small number would touch concussion exercise rehab. Per-total-headcount pricing would produce an absurd number and end the conversation.

**Seats** (CCM competency, one-off per clinician): NZ$895 → $425 at 40+. Unchanged; this one was justified on cost structure, not on customer revenue.

**Pilot: free for the first two organisations.** The reference is worth more than the fee.

## What we do not know, and must ask rather than model

- **Actual concussion episode volume** per supplier. Ask it in the first meeting; do not model it.
- Whether the tool is funded from supplier margin or is recoverable within the funded service.
- What each supplier currently spends per clinician on software.

**The episode economics below (item codes, rates, blended episode value) remain useful for understanding the customer's world and for the "what does this cost you per episode" conversation. The pricing conclusions drawn from them are withdrawn.**

# ★ THE PRICE (supersedes the banded base+variable card above)

**Structure decision (owner, 2026-07-20): a FLAT ANNUAL SUBSCRIPTION. Not per-episode.**

Per-episode was rejected for three reasons, all of which a procurement lead would raise:
1. **It requires the supplier to report episode volume to CEA** — administrative burden on the customer to be billed correctly, and a data flow nobody wants to negotiate.
2. **Procurement cannot budget a variable.** A line item that moves with caseload does not fit an annual budget approval; a fixed number does.
3. **Anything proportional to ACC payments weakens the contractual position.** Software licensing is defensible as procurement of an *input* under Standard T&Cs cl.16.1. A fee that scales with ACC billing invites the argument that CEA is taking a share of the funded service — which is the "subcontracting the Services" reading we need to avoid.

The episode economics still do the work: they *justify* the band, they are not *charged* per episode.

| Band | Concussion episodes/yr | **Annual subscription** | ≈ % of that band's exercise-related ACC billing |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Large** (multi-region) | 500+ | **NZ$22,000** | ~3.1% |

**One sentence to say it in:** *"It's a flat annual licence — twenty-two thousand for an organisation your size. That's about three percent of what you already bill ACC for the exercise-tolerance side of these episodes, and it doesn't move with your caseload."*

## Under the two funding scenarios

The funding question changes the *conversation*, not the price:
- **Funded from supplier margin** — lead with the flat annual number and the 3% framing. The point is that it is small, fixed, and budgetable.
- **Recoverable within the funded service** — the same number, but now framed against episode throughput: at $22,000 across 730 episodes it is **$30 per episode**, roughly 11 minutes of one TBI26 hour.

Either way you quote the same figure. **This removes the "we can't quote until we know" position from the supplier pack — it is obsolete.**

## Annual true-up

Offer unprompted: *"If your episode volume turns out to sit in a lower band, we move you down at renewal."* It costs nothing, it neutralises the fact that band assignment rests on modelled volume, and it signals the pricing is principled rather than extracted.

## READ THIS FIRST — what changed and why

The existing position models the NZ licence as **$3k or $30k depending on an unanswered question**. That is not a price. A 10× range tells a procurement lead one thing: the vendor has not done the arithmetic on their business.

This document does the arithmetic. The conclusion that matters:

> **The funding question changes the *shape* of the price, not the *level*.**
> Modelled properly against episode economics, the same supplier lands at **NZ$22,060/yr** under margin funding and **NZ$28,220/yr** under in-service recovery. That is a **1.28× spread, not 10×.** You do not need the funding answer to quote. You need it to decide how much of the price sits in fixed base versus variable per-episode.

**The pricing principle, in one sentence:** the licence is priced as a fixed percentage of the *exercise-tolerance-related ACC billing it supports* (TBI22 + TBI26), landing at **1.4%–2.9% of a supplier's total ACC concussion revenue** and **3.3%–6.7% of the exercise-related subset**, delivered as a small volume-banded base plus a per-episode fee.

# §3 — THE PRICING PRINCIPLE

## 3.1 The principle, stated

> **CEA prices the licence as a fixed fraction of the exercise-tolerance-related ACC billing it supports — targeting ~3% of TBI22 + TBI26 revenue, which lands at under 3% of the supplier's total ACC concussion revenue in every volume band and under 2% at scale.**
>
> **Delivered as: a small volume-banded annual base, plus a per-funded-episode fee, plus one-off per-seat competency certification.**

Three things this does that a flat licence cannot:

**It scales with the buyer's funded volume, not with their size.** A 1,400-clinician organisation and a 7-person clinic are not 200× apart on concussion episodes — they are 20× apart (730 vs 37 in the model). Price against clinician headcount and you overprice Active+ by an order of magnitude out of the market. Price against episodes and you track the thing the product is actually used for.

**It converts an uncertain benefit into a known unit cost.** A flat $18,000 licence asks a procurement lead to believe a benefit claim. **$16 per funded episode** asks them to believe arithmetic they can do themselves. The second is a far easier approval, and it is the reason per-episode beats flat even when the totals are identical.

**It de-risks the buyer's downside, which is the only risk they actually care about.** The failure mode a procurement lead is protecting against is *paying for something we then don't use.* A base-plus-variable structure caps that exposure at the base. Offer an annual true-up on actual episode counts (§6.3) and the exposure is capped at the base *permanently* — which costs CEA nothing and removes the single most common reason a small software deal dies in committee.

## 3.2 Why ~3% of touched billing is the right target

$29 at 3% of the blended $967 touched billing. Convert it to the unit the buyer thinks in:

```
$29 ÷ $155.27/hr = 0.187 hr = 11.2 minutes
```

> **At 3% of exercise-related billing, the tool costs about eleven minutes of one TBI26 hour per episode.**

That is the sentence the price is built to survive. Anything materially above ~5% starts to read as a claim on the service line rather than an input cost, and anything below ~1.5% does not fund a business or signal that the product is clinical infrastructure.

## 3.3 Why per-seat-plus-base and not per-seat alone

Because seats and episodes are uncorrelated at the top of the market, and per-seat-only is catastrophic there. See §7.1 — it is the single worst pricing mistake available in this channel.

| Tier 2 typical | 91 | 8 | 5–14 @ $695 | $5,560 | $4,502 | **$10,062** | 4.88% |

**Be honest about this table.** Year 1 including seats runs **3.0%–7.1%** of concussion revenue, and Tier 3 at 7.1% is the weakest number in the document.

Two answers, both true:

1. **Year 1 is the heaviest year by design.** Steady state is the licence alone: 1.4%–2.9%. Amortise the seats over a 3-year certification life and Active+'s all-in is **$32,180/yr = 1.95%**, Tier 3 is **$3,581/yr = 4.27%**.
2. **For Tier 3, offer the pilot instead (§4.5).** A 7-person clinic doing 37 episodes should not be sold a $5,968 year-one package. That is a pilot conversation, and pretending otherwise burns the reference.

## 4.5 Pilot pricing

The pilot exists to produce **a live NZ reference against ACC's Table 4 metrics** — the parallel-track rule in `NZ-ACC-TARGET-LIST.md` §7. Its job is not revenue.

| Pilot | Price | Covers | Term |
|---|---:|---|---|
| **Tier 1 / Tier 2 site pilot** | **$4,500** | one site, up to 25 funded episodes, 4 CCM seats | 90 days |
| **Tier 3 / single-clinic pilot** | **$1,800** | whole organisation, up to 25 episodes, 2 CCM seats | 90 days |

**Both are 100% creditable against the first year's contract if the supplier proceeds.**

**Justification for $4,500:** 25 episodes × $967 = **$24,175** of exercise-related billing observed during the pilot. $4,500 is **18.6%** of it — deliberately far above the 3% steady-state rate, because a pilot is a bounded onboarding-and-configuration cost that does not amortise. The credit clause makes the effective price **zero** on conversion, so the high per-episode rate prices the *option to walk away*, not the product.

**Justification for $1,800 at Tier 3:** it equals the Band E annual base. In effect, *year one is the pilot.* This is the right shape for Hemisphere Health, Tui Allied Health and Rehab Taranaki — no procurement layer, owner-clinician decides, and the number has to be small enough to say yes to in one conversation.

# §5 — THE TWO FUNDING SCENARIOS

## 5.1 The question to ask

Ask it in the first meeting, in this exact form:

> **"When you buy a clinical software tool or an outcome-measurement instrument for the Concussion Services contract — does that sit in your overheads, or is it costed into the service line you bill against TBI22 and TBI26?"**

And the confirming follow-up, which is the one that actually gets you the truth:

> **"Has ACC ever pushed back on a cost as non-recoverable under this contract?"**

The second question is better than the first because it asks about a *specific past event*, not a policy. People answer questions about events accurately and questions about policy aspirationally.

## 5.2 Scenario A — margin-funded

The supplier pays from its own margin against a fixed fee-for-service contract with a $4,120/client cap `[V]`. Finance-shaped buying committee. The comparison is against clinician hours saved.

**The correct response is not a lower price. It is a lower *fixed* price.** Under margin funding, the thing the buyer is protecting is fixed exposure against uncertain benefit. So shift weight off the base and onto the per-episode fee — the supplier only pays more when they are demonstrably using it more.

| Band | Base (Scenario A) | Per episode (Scenario A) |
|---|---:|---:|
| A (500+) | **$6,000** | **$22** |
| B (250–499) | **$4,000** | **$25** |
| C (100–249) | **$2,500** | **$28** |
| D (40–99) | **$1,500** | **$30** |
| E (under 40) | **$900** | **$32** |

**Recommended number, Active+:** `6,000 + 730 × 22` = **NZ$22,060/yr** — 1.33% of concussion revenue, $30.22/episode, **11.7 minutes of a TBI26 hour**.

**Recommended number, Tier 2 typical:** `1,500 + 91 × 30` = **NZ$4,230/yr** — 2.05%.

## 5.3 Scenario B — recoverable within the funded service

The tool is a service input, costed into delivery. Buying committee is clinical and contractual. The comparison is against the value of a defensible position when ACC assesses the cl.1.2.2 extension `[V]`.

Here the base can carry more weight, because a fixed service input is easier to budget than a variable one, and the buyer's risk is not fixed cost — it is capability.

| Band | Base (Scenario B) | Per episode (Scenario B) |
|---|---:|---:|
| A (500+) | **$18,000** | **$14** |
| B (250–499) | **$12,000** | **$15** |
| C (100–249) | **$7,000** | **$16** |
| D (40–99) | **$3,800** | **$18** |
| E (under 40) | **$2,200** | **$20** |

**Recommended number, Active+:** `18,000 + 730 × 14` = **NZ$28,220/yr** — 1.71% of concussion revenue, $38.66/episode, **14.9 minutes of a TBI26 hour**.

**Recommended number, Tier 2 typical:** `3,800 + 91 × 18` = **NZ$5,438/yr** — 2.64%.

## 5.4 The finding that kills the $3k–$30k range

| | Scenario A (margin) | Scenario B (recoverable) | **Spread** |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Active+ | **$22,060** | **$28,220** | **1.28×** |
| Habit Health | $14,950 | $18,570 | 1.24× |
| Tier 2 typical | $4,230 | $5,438 | 1.29× |
| Tier 3 typical | $2,084 | $2,940 | 1.41× |

*(Habit: A = 4,000 + 438×25 = $14,950; B = 12,000 + 438×15 = $18,570. Tier 3: A = 900 + 37×32 = $2,084; B = 2,200 + 37×20 = $2,940.)*

> **The funding answer moves the price by ~25–40%, not by 10×.**
>
> The old model produced a 10× range because it was anchored to *nothing*. Once the price is anchored to episode volume and exercise-related billing, the funding question stops setting the level — it only sets the **base/variable split**, which is a risk-shape decision, not a pricing decision.

**Operational consequence, and it is the most valuable line in this document:** the §Part 4 instruction in `NZ-ACC-SUPPLIER-PACK.md` — *"until that answer exists, quote nothing"* — **is now obsolete and should be retired.** You can quote in the first meeting.

The default to quote when the answer is unknown is **the §4.1 card**, which sits between the two scenarios. If the supplier subsequently says "margin", shift toward variable at the same total. If they say "recoverable", shift toward base at the same total. **Neither move is a discount, and neither is a climbdown** — which means you never have to reprice under pressure.

# §6 — OBJECTION MATH

Every calculation below is one a procurement lead will actually run. Have the answer before they finish the sentence.

## 6.1 "What does this cost us per client?"

**Their arithmetic:** $23,680 ÷ 730 = **$32.44 per episode**.

**Your answer:** *"$32 per funded episode. Your TBI26 rate is $155.27 an hour, so that's about twelve and a half minutes of one therapy hour. It's roughly three percent of the exercise-tolerance and exercise-programme billing on that episode, and it doesn't touch the $4,120 Maximum Funding Limit — it's a supplier input, not a service item, so it never competes with a client's funded care."*

The MFL clause matters. If a procurement lead thinks the cost comes out of the client's $4,120, the conversation is over. Pre-empt it.

## 6.2 "What's the payback?"

**Their arithmetic:** $23,680 ÷ $155.27/hr = **152.5 billable hours per year** across 730 episodes = **0.209 hr = 12.5 minutes per episode**.

**Your answer, in order of how defensible it is:**

**First — the unpaid admin, which is the strongest argument because it requires no clinical claim at all.** The contract is explicit: *"no additional fee may be invoiced for any report"* (cl.5.10, 5.13.6) `[V]`. Every ACC884 Client Summary Report is unpaid, due within 10 business days of Stage 1 commencing and again within 5 business days of exit `[V]`. Two per episode. If assembling the outcome-measurement content for an ACC884 takes 15 minutes today and the tool produces it, **the licence pays for itself on the first report of each episode and the second one is margin.** That claim needs no evidence about recovery time, only about report assembly time — and the supplier can check it against their own process in the meeting.

**Second — the six-monthly Supplier Report.** Referral volumes by source, median days from commencement to ACC884, goal achievement banded Fully/Partially/Minimally/None, **with a separate Māori cut** `[V]`. Almost certainly hand-assembled today. Ask: *"how many hours went into the report you filed this month?"* Whatever the number, it is against a $23,680 line, twice a year.

**Third — and only third — the RTW metrics.** All six ACC benchmarks are recovery-duration measures on ACC-side data `[V]`, and cl.13.4 means competitors see the results `[V]`. This is the strategic argument and the reason they will buy. **It is not the payback argument, because CEA cannot yet underwrite it with NZ outcome data.** Do not lead with it, and do not quantify it. Lead with the admin, which is arithmetic.

## 6.3 "What if our volume is at the low end?"

This is the objection that kills deals, and the answer is the strongest concession available.

**Their arithmetic:** *"You've modelled us at 730. Suppose we actually run 400."*

Band A → Band B. `8,000 + 400 × 18` = **$15,200**, or $38 per episode. The licence falls **36%** because 49% of it was variable.

**Your answer:** *"Then you pay $15,200, not $23,680. The per-episode component self-corrects automatically. And I'll write an annual true-up into the agreement: we set the band on your declared volume, and at twelve months we reconcile against actuals — if you came in a band low, you're credited the difference against the next year."*

**Offer the true-up unprompted.** It costs CEA nothing in a market this size, it removes the buyer's entire downside, and it converts the modelled volume numbers in §2 from *a thing you might be wrong about* into *a thing that self-corrects* — which neutralises the weakest part of the whole model.

The floor is the base. At Band E that is $1,500/yr, which is the honest answer to *"what if we do almost no concussion volume?"* — **then this is not for you, and I'd rather say so than sell you a licence you don't use.** In a market of 25 named accounts where everyone talks to everyone, that sentence is worth more than the deal.

## 6.4 "How do we know the volume figures are right?"

**Concede immediately.** *"I don't. ACC doesn't publish per-supplier referral volumes — I've modelled them from region coverage and organisation size, and I'd rather you corrected the number than accepted it. You know your episode count and I don't. Tell me and I'll band you on it."*

A vendor who has clearly modelled the buyer's economics and then hands over the assumption is in a materially stronger position than one who asserts. And the correction is exactly the information CEA needs.

## 6.5 "Why should we pay for training clinicians we already employ?"

**Their arithmetic:** *"60 seats at $425 is $25,500 — that's more than the software."*

**Your answer:** *"It is, in year one. It's one-off, the certification attaches to the individual, and it amortises over three years — so it's $8,500 a year against a $23,680 licence. What it buys is the thing an auditor asks for: you can demonstrate, per named clinician and on a date, that the people delivering the first-line protocol were trained in it. The contract obliges you to hold exactly those records — Operational Guidelines p.15, 'documentation of any additional certifications or specialised training completed'."* `[V]`

Then the structural point, which is the whole pitch: ACC mandates a five-discipline core team with **no exercise-testing competency in it** (cl.5.2.1) `[V]`, while obliging the supplier to assess exercise tolerance (cl.5.8.2.1.2) `[V]`. **The gap is closable by training, not by hiring** — and the alternative to a $425 seat is a role that does not have an ACC provider pathway at all (`NZ-ACC-PROCUREMENT-REALITY.md` §4: exercise physiologists are not among ACC's 22 recognised professions) `[V]`.

## 6.6 "Can we just buy the software and not the training?"

**No, and the reason is defensible rather than commercial.** The report is only as good as the clinician who signs it. A tool that produces a heart-rate-threshold prescription in the hands of a clinician who cannot defend the threshold derivation is a clinical risk to the supplier, not a benefit.

**But do not make this a hard blocker at Tier 3.** The pilot bundles 2 seats precisely so the question never gets asked.

## 6.7 "What's your discount?"

**There isn't one on the base or the per-episode rate**, and the reason is worth stating plainly: in a market of 25 named suppliers who are contractually shown each other's performance data (cl.13.4) `[V]`, a discount given to one is a discount owed to all within a quarter. Price integrity is a survival requirement here, not a stance.

What *is* available, and should be offered before it is asked for:

- **Term:** 24-month commitment at a 10% reduction on the base only (not per-episode). Costs CEA little, buys retention across the cl.1.2.2 extension decision.
- **Seat band:** commit to the next band up on seats and take that band's rate immediately.
- **Pilot credit:** 100% of the pilot fee against year one.
- **Staged seats:** seats phased by site over 12 months at the band rate for the *total committed*, not the first tranche.

# §7 — WHAT NOT TO DO

## 7.1 ⛔ Never price per-clinician-seat only

Active+ has **1,400+ clinicians** `[V]`. Habit Health has **2,500+** `[V]`.

```
1,400 × $425 = $595,000
```

That is **36% of their entire modelled ACC concussion revenue**, for a service line most of those clinicians never touch. The modelled concussion-delivering subset is **~60 people — 4.3% of headcount.**

This is the mistake the old $3k/$30k model was one step away from making, and it fails in both directions: catastrophically overpriced at Active+, and at Hemisphere Health (7 staff) a per-seat-only model yields **$3,000–6,000** for an organisation running 37 episodes — priced out of a market they should be the fastest close in.

**Headcount is uncorrelated with concussion volume. Never price against it.**

## 7.2 ⛔ Never contract as a share or percentage of ACC funding

Use "% of ACC concussion revenue" as **an internal justification and a verbal sanity check only**. It must never appear as a pricing *mechanism* in a quote, proposal or agreement.

Two distinct risks:

**Contractual.** Standard T&Cs cl.16.1: *"You must not enter into a contract with someone else to deliver any part of the Services without ACC's written approval."* `[V]` The whole strategy rests on the reading that licensing software is procurement of an *input*, not subcontracting the Services (`NZ-ACC-PROCUREMENT-REALITY.md` §1) — and that reading is itself flagged as inference, not verified ACC position. **A fee expressed as a percentage of ACC payments is the single fastest way to make that reading indefensible.** It would look like participation in the funded service.

**Reputational.** A vendor fee indexed to publicly funded injury compensation reads badly in any forum it is ever quoted in, and this is a market where cl.13.4 guarantees suppliers talk to each other `[V]`.

**Contract in fixed dollar units: a stated base and a stated dollar amount per funded episode.** Both are supplier inputs. Neither is a share of anything.

## 7.3 ⛔ Never quote a range

"$3,000 to $30,000" tells a procurement lead you have not modelled their business. Quote **one number, with the arithmetic behind it**, and let the band adjust when they correct the volume. A number you can defend beats a range every time, even if the number is wrong — because a wrong number invites a correction and a range invites a dismissal.

## 7.4 ⛔ Never price on site count, "users", or region coverage

All uncorrelated with funded episodes. Habit has 125 locations `[V]` and is modelled at 60% of Active+'s volume. Advantage South covers 3 regions with an unpublished headcount. Region coverage was used in §2 to *estimate* volume in the absence of data — it is not a billing unit.

## 7.5 ⛔ Never offer a flat national licence

A single flat price fails at both ends simultaneously: at $12,000 it is 14% of Tier 3's concussion revenue (unsellable), and at $2,500 it is 0.15% of Active+'s (leaving ~$21,000 on the table at the one account that matters most). The 20:1 volume spread across the market makes banding structural, not optional.

## 7.6 ⛔ Never tie price to ACC KPI outcomes

No gainshare, no outcome-linked fee, no at-risk pricing on the Table 4 metrics.

Three reasons. **The contract has no mechanism** — payment is strictly fee-for-service with no outcome-linked component, bonus or gainshare `[V]`, so the supplier has no funding stream to pay a success fee from. **CEA cannot underwrite the clinical claim** — there is no NZ outcome data yet, and pricing against RTW duration is a claim you would have to defend. **And the metrics are ACC-computed from ACC-side data** `[V]` — CEA could not verify its own invoice.

## 7.7 ⛔ Never quote in AUD, and never quote incl GST

Every ACC item price is excl GST `[V]`. Quote **NZD, excl GST**, and state the GST treatment explicitly for a non-resident supplier. Quoting A$ makes the buyer do FX arithmetic to evaluate you and signals the offer was ported rather than built.

## 7.8 ⛔ Never discount the base to close

Discount the **term**, the **seat band**, or credit the **pilot** (§6.7). A base discount at one of 25 suppliers who see each other's data is a base discount at all 25 within a quarter.

## 7.9 ⛔ Do not present §2's volume figures as researched

They are modelled from region coverage and organisation size. `NZ-ACC-TARGET-LIST.md` §8.4 is explicit that no per-supplier volume data is published and that an **OIA request to ACC** is the route to real numbers. Present §2 as a model, invite correction (§6.4), **and file the OIA** — real per-supplier volumes would replace the weakest section of this document with fact and let CEA band every account correctly before the first meeting.
