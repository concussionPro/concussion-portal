## POSITIONING SPINE — the one differentiator, and the frame it sits in

**The frame.** Amsterdam 2022 moved concussion from a **rest** condition to an **exercise-prescription** condition, gated on exercise testing and on an individually derived heart-rate threshold. Prescribed individualised exercise shortened median recovery from 17 days to 13 days in the pivotal randomised trial (Leddy et al., *JAMA Pediatrics* 2019; n=103 adolescent athletes aged 13-18, P=.009), and reduces the incidence of persisting symptoms. Every relevant profession was trained **before** that shift. A survey of **1,272 physiotherapists** found **70% already held concussion training** and averaged **13 out of 15** on knowledge — with the specific gaps in return-to-play confidence and in knowing **when graded exertional training is indicated**.

**The line to land: practitioners are not ignorant — they are current on the old model.** CRM is the update to the new model. Never frame it as remedial education or as fixing ignorance. In Canada that framing would be both insulting and false, because the market is already served by established concussion education.

**The one genuine differentiator, and the only one to argue.** Canadian concussion training in this space is predominantly **assessment-oriented**: recognition, sideline evaluation, screening, referral, return-to-sport staging. CRM's distinct contribution is the layer beneath the staging — **heart-rate-threshold exercise prescription**: BCTT/BCBT-derived threshold identification, conversion of that threshold into a complete FITT prescription, real-time symptom-response monitoring, and re-test-driven progression of the physiological ceiling. That is the exercise-physiology treatment layer, and assessment-oriented concussion training does not teach it.

**The supporting card.** CSEP already grants professional equivalency to ESSA's AEP and AES credentials. CRM is written to the ESSA AEP scope of practice, so its competency boundaries sit inside a framework CSEP has already assessed and accepted. This is a review-simplification argument, not a superiority argument — present it that way.

**What not to claim.** Do not claim CRM is the only or the leading concussion training available to Canadian practitioners. Do not claim a gap in Canadian provision generally. The claim is narrow and defensible: an exercise-prescription layer that assessment-oriented training does not cover, written to a scope framework CSEP already recognises.

# APPLICATION CONTENT

## 1. Provider

Concussion Education Australia (CEA) is a specialist provider of concussion education for health and exercise professionals. Rather than offer broad general CPD, CEA publishes to depth in a single condition: every programme concerns the recognition, assessment, rehabilitation and return-to-activity management of concussion and mild traumatic brain injury. Programmes are authored in-house by a registered clinician, referenced to the primary peer-reviewed literature, and written to explicit scope-of-practice boundaries so non-medical practitioners can act confidently within their lane and escalate correctly outside it.

## 2. Course description

**Concussion Rehab Mastery** trains exercise professionals to deliver the exercise-based treatment of concussion that international consensus now places first-line.

The paradigm changed. Concussion management used to mean rest until symptoms resolved; the evidence overturned that, and the Amsterdam 2022 consensus statement made sub-symptom-threshold aerobic exercise — prescribed from a heart-rate threshold derived by graded exercise testing — the first-line treatment. That change put the centre of concussion rehabilitation into exercise-physiology practice.

The course covers, across eight modules: the pathophysiology that explains why exercise works and why it must stay below threshold; recognition and red-flag triage; the complete Buffalo Concussion Treadmill Test and Buffalo Concussion Bike Test protocols; conversion of the heart-rate threshold into a full FITT prescription; phenotype-specific rehabilitation across the seven recognised concussion phenotypes; the six-stage graded return-to-sport strategy with reconditioning physiology and load monitoring; persistent post-concussion symptoms and the programme that stalls; and the documentation and inter-professional communication that close the loop with the referring clinician.

**Scope discipline is a design principle, not a disclaimer.** Concussion diagnosis is a medical act. Return-to-contact clearance is a medical act. The practitioner trained by this course implements graded exertion, measures it, documents it, escalates red flags, and returns a prepared athlete to the physician who holds the clearance decision. Learners are assessed on that boundary as rigorously as on the protocol itself.

## 5. Assessment

Assessment is **distributed and per-module**, not a single terminal exam.

- **87 assessment questions** across the eight modules, allocated in proportion to module weight.
- Delivered **within each module**, at the point of learning, so a learner cannot advance past a protocol step they have not understood.
- **80% pass mark** at module level.
- Items test protocol accuracy (per-minute BCTT increments, the two distinct termination criteria), physiological reasoning (why the symptom threshold marks an autonomic and cerebrovascular ceiling), clinical decision-making (progress, regress, refer) and scope discipline, including red-flag escalation and the boundary at return-to-contact clearance.
- Each item returns a written rationale on answer.
- Certification requires every module at or above the pass mark.

- **Exercise professionals in return-to-work and insurer-funded rehabilitation**, where post-concussion exercise intolerance is a frequent and poorly managed barrier to sustainable return.

**Assumed prior knowledge:** undergraduate-level exercise physiology — cardiorespiratory response to exercise, heart-rate and RPE monitoring, graded exercise test principles, FITT programming. The course does not teach these; it applies them to concussion.

## 7. Learning outcomes

The course carries **51 assessed learning objectives**, reproduced in full below as delivered.

### Module 1 — Concussion for the Exercise Physiologist
- Define concussion as a functional brain injury, distinguish it from structural traumatic brain injury, and explain why standard neuroimaging is typically normal.
- Describe the biomechanical mechanisms of concussion — rotational, linear, coup-contrecoup and diffuse axonal injury — and explain why rotational forces are considered most damaging.
- Trace the neurometabolic cascade from mechanoporation through ionic flux, Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase overdrive, hyperglycolysis, Ca²⁺-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction and neuroinflammation to recovery — and connect each phase to clinical symptoms.
- Explain cerebral blood-flow dysregulation after concussion, including impaired autoregulation, neurovascular uncoupling and reduced cerebrovascular CO₂ reactivity, and describe how each contributes to symptom provocation during exertion.
- Describe post-concussion autonomic dysfunction — sympathovagal imbalance, blunted baroreflex sensitivity and reduced HRV — and explain the mechanism of exercise intolerance.
- Justify why sub-symptom-threshold aerobic exercise is now first-line care rather than prolonged rest, citing the evidence base that drove the paradigm shift.
- Locate the exercise physiologist clearly within the multidisciplinary concussion team as the implementer of graded exertion, not the diagnostician or the clearer.

### Module 2 — Recognition, Red Flags & Scope of Practice
- Identify the observable signs and reported symptom clusters of concussion, and explain the neurophysiological mechanism underlying each cluster.
- List the red flags that mandate urgent emergency escalation, and describe the pathophysiological mechanism — intracranial haemorrhage, second-impact syndrome and cervical spine injury — that makes each a medical emergency requiring immediate emergency services activation.
- Articulate the scope boundary in concussion with its medico-legal rationale: what the exercise physiologist can and cannot do, and why diagnosis and medical clearance require medical authority.
- Describe how SCAT6 and SCOAT6 function as serial outcome measures within scope, and identify the critical limits of these tools that prevent them from being used for diagnosis or clearance by a non-medical practitioner.
- Navigate referral-in and referral-out pathways: describe how a concussion case appropriately reaches the exercise physiologist, recognise when symptoms warrant routing to vestibular physiotherapy, a sports physician, psychology or neurology, and describe the practitioner's position as active-rehabilitation specialist in the multidisciplinary team.

### Module 3 — Assessment That Is the Treatment (BCTT/BCBT)
- Explain the exercise-physiology rationale for a graded exertion protocol in concussion — specifically why a near-linear cardiovascular ramp identifies the autonomic and cerebrovascular ceiling that characterises the physiological phenotype.
- Identify all pre-test contraindications and red flags, categorise them as absolute or relative, and describe the correct response to each.
- Administer the standardised Balke-based Buffalo Concussion Treadmill Test (BCTT), including the precise per-minute incline and speed increments, continuous heart rate/RPE/symptom monitoring, and the two distinct test-termination criteria.
- Record the heart rate at symptom threshold (HRt), describe what it represents physiologically (the point where impaired cerebral autoregulation and blunted cerebrovascular CO₂ reactivity can no longer meet exertional demand), and distinguish it from the voluntary-exhaustion endpoint.
- Select and administer the Buffalo Concussion Bike Test (BCBT) when the treadmill protocol is contraindicated or clinically suboptimal, and describe the validated protocol steps.
- Convert the HRt into an individualised sub-symptom-threshold aerobic prescription using the 80–90% rule, including frequency, duration and mode.
- Apply a structured re-testing schedule to progress the symptom-threshold ceiling, identify the physiological-recovery endpoint, and communicate findings to the referring clinician within scope.
- Use VOMS, BESS and timed tandem gait as serial adjunct outcome measures to track vestibular, ocular and postural-control recovery — distinguishing their role from diagnostic assessment.

### Module 4 — Sub-Symptom-Threshold Aerobic Rehabilitation
- Explain the full biochemical and physiological rationale for sub-symptom-threshold aerobic exercise — covering autonomic restoration, cerebral autoregulation and CO₂ reactivity normalisation, exercise-induced BDNF upregulation and neuroplasticity, neuroinflammation reduction, and systemic deconditioning reversal.
- Describe the heart-rate threshold (HRt) as the physiological marker of the autonomic/cerebrovascular ceiling, and explain why training at 80–90% of HRt (rather than at or above it) produces adaptive recovery.
- Derive a complete FITT prescription from a BCTT HRt result, including worked calculations of the target HR band.
- Apply the real-time symptom-monitoring rule (≥2-point rise = stop/reduce), distinguishing acceptable baseline noise from genuine threshold-crossing.
- Progress the program safely using the re-test-driven progression algorithm — stepping the HR ceiling as tolerance recovers, and distinguishing the "duration first, then intensity" sequencing rule.
- Select appropriate modalities for the deconditioned, dizzy, anxious or otherwise complex patient, and adapt the prescription to the presentation.
- Recognise red flags that require regression, pause or urgent escalation, and communicate these to the referrer within scope.

### Module 5 — Phenotype-Specific Exercise Rehabilitation
- Describe the neurophysiological mechanism underlying each of the seven concussion phenotypes (vestibular, oculomotor, cervicogenic, autonomic/physiologic, cognitive/fatigue, affective, headache/migraine) and connect each mechanism to its clinical presentation.
- Prescribe, dose and progress the in-scope exercise rehabilitation for each phenotype — including VOR×1/VOR×2 gaze stabilisation, habituation and dynamic balance for the vestibular phenotype; convergence, saccade, pursuit and accommodative facility drills for the oculomotor phenotype; cranio-cervical flexion, joint-position-error retraining and postural endurance work for the cervicogenic phenotype; and sub-symptom-threshold aerobic exercise for the autonomic phenotype.
- Explain the cerebellar neuroplasticity mechanism by which VOR gaze-stabilisation exercises restore vestibular function, and distinguish central vestibular adaptation from peripheral otoconia repositioning.
- Describe the cervico-ocular reflex and explain how cervical proprioceptive injury contributes to dizziness and gaze instability after concussion.
- Apply symptom-titrated dosing and a three-phase progression framework across all phenotypes, including the criteria for advancing, regressing and referring.
- Identify the dominant phenotype driver when multiple phenotypes co-present, sequence the treatment plan accordingly, and layer secondary phenotype exercises appropriately.
- Recognise the presentations — BPPV, manifest tropia, clinical depression, vertebrobasilar red flags — that require referral before or instead of exercise, and identify the appropriate specialist for each.

### Module 6 — Graded Return to Activity, Sport & Performance
- Apply the international graded return-to-sport (GRTS) strategy as a staged, symptom-monitored progression — with particular depth on stages 3 and 4 and what distinguishes each from the preceding stage.
- Comply with mandated stand-down requirements and correctly apply both minimums (calendar stand-down and symptom-free period) to a real case, identifying which governs.
- Explain the physiology of reconditioning — why aerobic base must precede sport-specific load, and the specific mechanisms by which running, multidirectional movement and contact-preparation each introduce qualitatively new physiological demands.
- Design a concussion-specific return-to-running progression and team-sport conditioning program, applying objective progress, regress and readiness criteria governed by the 24-hour symptom rule.
- Coordinate physical and cognitive load demands (return-to-learn and return-to-work pacing) and communicate an athlete's reconditioning readiness to the medical practitioner responsible for contact clearance.

### Module 7 — Persistent Symptoms & the Complex Case
- Define persistent post-concussion symptoms (PPCS) using the current consensus timeframe and terminology, and explain why PPCS is a heterogeneous clinical picture rather than a single diagnosis.
- Describe the five pathophysiological mechanisms that drive persistence beyond the acute phase: sustained autonomic and cerebrovascular dysregulation, unresolved neuroinflammation, central sensitisation, the deconditioning spiral, and psychological maintaining factors including fear-avoidance and catastrophising.
- Identify the clinical and demographic risk factors that predict prolonged recovery, and explain why early activity avoidance is itself a modifiable, iatrogenic risk factor.
- Justify, from the evidence base, why graded sub-symptom-threshold aerobic exercise remains indicated — and actively therapeutic — in PPCS when a genuine symptom-limited threshold is confirmed.
- Apply a four-step structured decision framework when a rehabilitation program stalls: reassess the BCTT threshold, screen for non-aerobic drivers, re-refer with data, and escalate red flags immediately.
- Adapt rehabilitation for the comorbid, deconditioned and anxious patient using pacing, graded exposure and behaviour-change strategies within scope.
- Recognise presentations that require referral beyond the exercise-physiologist lane — including progressive presentations suggesting neurodegenerative concern — and describe what an accurate, in-scope response looks like in practice.

### Module 8 — Documentation, Communication & Referral
- Produce the four core in-scope concussion-rehabilitation documents: assessment/BCTT record, session and progression tracking sheet, practitioner-to-referrer communication letter, and return-to-activity progress note — each as a real, structured artifact with scope-appropriate language.
- Apply the record-within-scope principle: frame findings as exercise-physiology observations and recommendations, never as a diagnosis or medical clearance for return to contact.
- Explain the three jobs every clinical record performs simultaneously — clinical continuity, inter-professional communication, and medico-legal accountability — and recognise why all three apply to concussion records.
- Apply the privacy principles governing the health information collected, stored and disclosed in the course of concussion rehabilitation, including the obligations triggered by AI scribe tools.
- Use AI-assisted documentation tools as an efficiency aid while retaining full personal professional responsibility for the accuracy and scope-appropriateness of every clinical note filed.

## 9. Canadian context

**Return-to-play legislation and guidance.** Module 6 teaches a jurisdiction-neutral competency: identify the mandated stand-down requirements that apply to the athlete in front of you, determine which minimum governs, refuse to progress the athlete past the stage the governance permits, and hand the contact-clearance decision to the physician.
