## SECTION 2 — WHAT "SUB-THRESHOLD" ACTUALLY MEANS (2:30–7:00)

[SLIDE: symptom-threshold curve — exertion on x, symptoms on y, threshold point marked]

Start with the physiology, because the phrase "sub-symptom-threshold" gets used loosely.

After concussion, autonomic regulation is disturbed — cerebral blood-flow control, exercise tolerance, heart-rate response. The clinical expression: a patient exerts, and at some *reproducible* intensity their symptoms rise. Below that intensity, they can exercise without symptom exacerbation. That crossover point is the symptom threshold — and crucially, it's *individual* and it's *measurable*, expressed as a heart rate.

[SLIDE: "The threshold is a heart rate — HRt"]

The Buffalo group formalised this: a graded exertion test finds the heart rate at which symptoms begin to increase — the threshold heart rate, HRt. Prescription follows from measurement: aerobic exercise at a fixed percentage *below* that threshold, progressed as the threshold itself recovers.

Two things about that sentence do all the work.

First — **the dose is anchored to a measured number, not a formula.** Age-predicted formulas tell you about a population. Your patient is not a population. Two 24-year-olds, same sport, same injury date — thresholds can be 40 beats apart.

Second — **the threshold is the outcome measure.** As the patient recovers, the threshold climbs. Serial measurement gives you a physiological recovery trajectory — a number that moves as the brain's autonomic control recovers. Not "how are your symptoms this week." A number.

[SLIDE: why this beats symptom scores — bullets]

Hold onto that, because it's the spine of everything else in this session: measured threshold in, measured trajectory out. Symptom scores are subjective, effort-dependent, and — for anyone managing return-to-play — carry a known incentive problem: athletes under-report to get back sooner. A heart-rate threshold is much harder to perform.

## SECTION 3 — HOW A THRESHOLD IS MEASURED (7:00–13:00)

[SLIDE: BCTT — the reference standard]

The reference standard is the Buffalo Concussion Treadmill Test. Walk on a treadmill at a fixed speed; the incline increases one *degree* each minute — degree, not percent, and yes, that distinction has ruined more than one home-built protocol. Each minute: rate of perceived exertion, symptom rating. The test stops at symptom exacerbation — a defined rise above baseline — or at volitional exhaustion with an RPE above 17.

The heart rate at the stopping point is the threshold. Prescription: aerobic sessions at **80 to 90 percent of that measured HRt**, classically around 20 minutes, most days of the week — that's the dosing architecture from the Buffalo protocols that the trials ran.

[SLIDE: what the BCTT asks of a clinic]

Now — the honest clinic-floor audit. The BCTT wants a treadmill, a calibrated protocol, twenty-plus minutes of a clinician's undivided attention, and a patient who tolerates a lab-style test. There's a bike variant for when a treadmill's wrong. It is an excellent test. It is also, for a busy MSK clinic seeing two or three concussions a month, a genuine operational cost — a full appointment slot spent producing one number.

And here's what the survey data tells us happens under that cost: exposure predicts practice. Dobney's data showed clinicians with high concussion caseloads — 75 to 100 percent of their week — were significantly more likely to prescribe aerobic exercise. The clinicians running concussion clinics absorbed the measurement cost. Everyone else fell back on what fits in a standard consult: education, sleep advice, "stay under your symptoms" — the treatment gap, operationalised.

[SLIDE: "The gap is operational, not intellectual"]

Which reframes the problem. Two-thirds of clinicians aren't ignorant of exercise therapy. They're rationally declining a workflow their clinic can't carry. If measurement were cheap — if the graded test ran on the patient's own device, guided, minute by minute, with the threshold falling out the end — the calculus changes completely.

That's not hypothetical anymore. Wearable heart-rate technology made guided, structured graded testing possible outside the lab — the same staged exertion, symptom check each minute, stop criteria applied, threshold captured. What was an appointment becomes a protocol.

## SECTION 4 — DELIVERY: WHERE HOME REHAB GOES DARK (13:00–19:00)

[SLIDE: "The prescription is 20 minutes, most days — at home"]

Say you've measured. HRt 152. You prescribe: twenty minutes, 122 to 137 — 80 to 90 percent — most days. The patient goes home.

Now the second gap opens, and it's wider than the first: **you have no idea what happens next.**

[SLIDE: three failure modes — bullets]

Three failure modes, and every clinician here has seen all of them:

One — **overshoot.** The motivated athlete treats the band as a floor. They train at 160 because it "felt fine," flare for two days, and arrive at review convinced exercise makes them worse. The treatment gets blamed for its own overdose.

Two — **undershoot.** The anxious patient — often the one with the most autonomic symptoms — walks gently, never approaches the band, gets no training stimulus, doesn't progress, and concludes exercise doesn't work. The treatment gets blamed for never being delivered.

Three — **the reporting veil.** At review you ask: how did the week go? You get memory, filtered through personality. "Pretty good." You're titrating a physiological dose off "pretty good."

[SLIDE: "Dose and adherence: observed, not assumed"]

Contrast any other measured therapy. We'd never titrate insulin off "pretty good." The fix is the same one measurement gave us at testing: the patient's own wearable, every session, live against the prescribed band — time-in-band, drift above, symptom check attached to the session, flare events visible the day they happen rather than reconstructed a week later.

Two protocol details from the evidence base matter enormously here, and they're both counter-intuitive enough that unsupervised patients get them wrong:

**Progress by heart rate, not by duration.** The instinct is "you managed 20 minutes, let's go to 30." The protocol holds duration and *raises the band* as the threshold recovers — because the threshold is the thing healing.

**After a flare: reduce, don't rest.** A symptom spike prompts most patients — and plenty of clinicians — straight back to rest. The protocol drops intensity and continues. Deconditioning is the enemy; retreat feeds it.

And one prognostic flag worth knowing from the Buffalo work: a very low initial threshold — under about 135 — identifies patients at elevated risk of prolonged recovery. That number, on day one, tells you who needs closer management. You only get it if you measured.

[SLIDE: serial HRt plot — climbing across weeks, fabricated example, labelled as such]

Put measurement at the start and verification in the middle, and something valuable falls out the end: the episode documents itself.

This is a fabricated example episode — no real patient. Threshold at intake: 138. Re-test day nine: 149. Day nineteen: 164, test terminated on exhaustion criteria, not symptoms. Under it: every home session, date-stamped, time-in-band, symptom deltas. That's not my notes about recovery. That's recovery, *recorded*.

[SLIDE: "Return-to-play is the decision clinicians trust least — Yorke 2016"]

Why this matters beyond tidy notes: in a survey of over 1,200 physical therapists — seventy percent with prior concussion training — the task they reported *least* confidence in wasn't assessment, wasn't referral. It was return-to-play. The decision with the medico-legal weight attached. Confidence follows from a measurement you can point to. "Symptom-free and back to baseline threshold with three clean verified weeks" is a defensible clinical position — in the file, to the GP, to the insurer, to a tribunal if it ever comes to that.

For those of you working funded caseloads — insurer treatment requests, progress reporting, NDIS goals — you already know the other half of this: reports assembled from appointment notes at the deadline, in hours nobody pays for. ## SECTION 6 — WHAT THIS LOOKS LIKE IN A REAL WEEK (24:00–28:30)

[SLIDE: the week, patient-side — phone/watch frames]

Let me compress everything into one ordinary case. Fabricated, typical.

Twenty-six-year-old netballer, day twelve post-injury, symptomatic at training, referred in. Guided graded test — her own watch — stops on symptom criteria at 141. Prescription auto-derived: 113 to 127, twenty minutes, most days. Under 135 at intake? No — but close enough to schedule the re-test early.

Week one: five sessions, four clean. Session three drifts high for four minutes — she felt good — the same-day view catches it, and the flare that follows gets its correct interpretation: overdose, not deterioration. Reduce, don't rest. Week two re-test: 155. Band moves up. She can *see* the number climbing — and I'll tell you from my own caseload, that visible number does more for adherence than any education handout I've ever given: the treatment stops being "walk gently and hope" and becomes a score she's beating.

Week four: threshold back over 180, terminated on exhaustion, symptom-free through graduated loading. The return-to-play conversation happens over a trajectory chart, not a feelings recap. The GP letter renders from the episode. Total additional clinician time versus "prescribe and hope": close to zero. Total additional *information*: everything.
