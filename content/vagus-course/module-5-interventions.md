# Module 5 — Defensible Interventions

---

This module ranks vagal-related interventions by evidence quality. The honest version, not the wellness-marketing version.

[KEYPOINT: The strength-of-evidence pyramid for vagal interventions: vagal manoeuvres for tachyarrhythmia (RCT-supported) → slow-paced breathing (meta-analytic support) → graded exercise post-concussion (RCT-supported) → manual therapy (limited evidence) → wellness products (no robust evidence).]

## Tier 1 — Evidence-supported in acute clinical context

### Modified Valsalva for SVT

The REVERT trial (Smith et al., 2015, Lancet) demonstrated postural-modified Valsalva is significantly more effective than standard Valsalva for restoring sinus rhythm in supraventricular tachycardia.

**Protocol:** Patient semi-recumbent → strain (40 mmHg manometric pressure or proxy for 15 seconds) → immediately laid supine with passive leg raise to 45° for 15 seconds.

**Use case:** Acute SVT in primary care or ED settings. Within scope for clinicians with appropriate training.

## Tier 2 — Meta-analytic support for symptom management

### Slow-paced breathing (resonant frequency breathing)

Laborde et al. (2022) — meta-analysis of voluntary slow breathing on HRV demonstrates reliable acute effects on HRV indices and subjective state.

**Protocol:** 5-6 breaths per minute (5-second inhale, 5-second exhale) for 10-20 minutes daily.

**Use case:** Adjunct for anxiety, mild orthostatic intolerance, chronic-pain coping. Not a primary intervention.

**Honest caveat:** The HRV effect is reliable; the downstream clinical outcomes (anxiety reduction, BP improvement, sleep) are more variable.

### Graded sub-symptom-threshold exercise (post-concussion)

Leddy et al. (2019) RCT supports early sub-symptom-threshold aerobic exercise for sport-related concussion recovery.

**Protocol:** Buffalo Concussion Treadmill Test → personalised heart-rate target at 80% of symptom-onset threshold → progressive aerobic protocol with weekly reassessment.

**Use case:** Acute and subacute post-concussion autonomic dysfunction with appropriate clinical oversight.

## Tier 3 — Mechanistic plausibility, limited clinical RCTs

### Manual therapy (osteopathic, physiotherapy)

Suboccipital release, sternocleidomastoid soft-tissue techniques, and cervical mobilisation are sometimes framed as "vagal stimulation."

**The honest position:** Anatomical plausibility exists (the vagus emerges from the jugular foramen and traverses the cervical fascia). Specific manual-technique RCTs targeting autonomic outcomes are limited and underpowered.

**Use case:** Adjunct in a broader treatment plan when there's a separate musculoskeletal indication (e.g., cervicogenic component to symptoms). Do not bill the intervention specifically as "vagal toning."

### Cold exposure / cold-water face immersion

Diving reflex is well-described (cold-water face immersion → bradycardia + peripheral vasoconstriction). Some emergency-medicine use for tachyarrhythmia termination.

**Use case:** Acute SVT termination as a low-tech alternative to Valsalva when the patient cannot perform Valsalva. Routine "cold plunge for vagal health" lacks specific RCT support.

[REDFLAG: Cold exposure has acute cardiac risk in patients with undiagnosed structural heart disease, severe hypertension, or recent acute cardiac events. Screen for cardiac risk before recommending cold-water immersion protocols.]

## Tier 4 — Insufficient evidence for routine clinical recommendation

These products and protocols are widely marketed but lack the clinical evidence for routine recommendation:

- Direct-to-consumer transcutaneous auricular VNS devices (early-stage evidence)
- "Vagal tone" supplements (no specific mechanism, no RCT support)
- Humming, gargling, and singing protocols marketed as vagal toning (mechanism plausible, clinical-outcome data thin)
- Polyvagal-informed therapy (heterogeneous practice, hard to evaluate as a single intervention — see Module 1)

**The honest clinical position:** Patients may report benefit from these. You don't have to debunk them — but you should not prescribe them as evidence-based interventions, and you should not bill them under that framing.
