# Module 3 — Clinical Assessment

---

This module covers what you can actually measure in a 20-minute consult and what you should reserve for referral or instrumented testing.

[KEYPOINT: The bedside autonomic exam answers more diagnostic questions than any HRV device. Master the bedside exam before reaching for a wearable.]

## The bedside autonomic exam (10 minutes, no equipment)

The active stand test is the single most useful bedside procedure for vagally-framed presentations.

**Protocol:**

- Patient supine for 5-10 minutes. Take BP and HR at minute 5 and minute 10 (averaged).
- Patient stands. Take BP and HR at minute 1, minute 3, and minute 5 of standing.

**Interpretation:**

| Pattern | Likely diagnosis |
|---|---|
| Sustained HR increase ≥30 bpm (≥40 in adolescents) without orthostatic hypotension | POTS (provisional — needs cardiology confirmation) |
| BP drop ≥20 mmHg systolic or ≥10 mmHg diastolic within 3 min | Orthostatic hypotension |
| Sustained tachycardia ≥100 bpm at rest, not orthostatic | Inappropriate sinus tachycardia (IST) — refer |
| BP and HR within normal ranges | Symptoms not orthostatic — consider other phenotypes |

## What HRV actually measures (and what it does not)

[DEFINITION: HRV | Heart Rate Variability — the variation in time between successive heartbeats. Measured in milliseconds (typically RMSSD, SDNN, pNN50) or via frequency-domain analysis (HF, LF, LF/HF ratio).]

[INFOGRAPHIC: hrv-honest-ranking]

**What HRV reliably correlates with:**
- General cardiovascular health (population-level)
- Acute autonomic state under controlled conditions (short-term measurement)
- Recovery status in athletes (when measured consistently at the same time each day)

**What HRV does not reliably measure:**
- "Vagal tone" as a static trait (the term is contested — see Module 1)
- Trauma-related state changes (claims that exceed the data)
- Anxiety severity in absolute terms
- Whether a patient "needs" a vagal intervention

## When objective autonomic testing is justified

Tilt-table testing, autonomic-function lab studies, and 24-hour Holter monitoring are appropriate when:

- The bedside active-stand test is positive but the diagnosis is uncertain
- Syncope is recurrent, unexplained, or potentially cardiac
- Suspected POTS where treatment will be initiated and the diagnosis affects the regimen
- The patient is being assessed for chronic disability claim or workers comp

These are specialist-level investigations. Refer to a cardiologist or neurologist with an autonomic interest — they are listed on most state Health Department websites.

[REDFLAG: A wearable-derived HRV drop alone is not an indication for tilt-table testing, dietary supplements, or "vagal-tone protocols." Treat the patient, not the device.]

## The five-minute write-up

After the bedside exam, your note should contain:

- Active stand findings (numerical, all three readings)
- Symptom pattern that prompted the assessment
- Differential diagnosis (Module 2 categories)
- Plan: refer / observe / intervene
- If intervening, specific intervention (Module 5) and what would prompt escalation

[TRYTHIS: Add the active-stand test to your standard intake for any patient presenting with "dizziness, fatigue, palpitations, or vagal-coded language." Takes 10 minutes. Reframes most consultations within that window.]
