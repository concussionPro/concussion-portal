## Before You Begin: Where the EP Sits in Concussion Care

[KEYPOINT: This course trains you to IMPLEMENT concussion exercise rehabilitation. As an Accredited Exercise Physiologist (AEP), you do NOT diagnose concussion and you do NOT medically clear an athlete to return to sport. Diagnosis, red-flag triage and clearance are the responsibility of medical practitioners (GPs, sports physicians) and, within their scope, physiotherapists. Your value is in the safe, graded, objectively-monitored delivery of exertion that those clinicians cannot supervise at the same dose or frequency.]

Concussion management has become genuinely multidisciplinary, and exercise physiology is now central to it rather than peripheral. The reason is simple: the most evidence-supported active treatment for concussion — sub-symptom-threshold aerobic exercise — is precisely the intervention EPs are trained to prescribe, dose and progress.

Throughout this course, the boundary is consistent. A patient arrives in your care with an existing concussion diagnosis and a referral. You assess exercise tolerance, prescribe and progress a graded program, monitor symptom and physiological responses, and communicate back to the referring clinician. If you observe deterioration or red flags, you stop and escalate — you do not re-diagnose, and you do not sign off return-to-play.

Understanding the underlying pathophysiology is not academic. It is what lets you explain to a patient why their heart rate is being capped below the point most gym-goers would consider "a workout," why pushing through symptoms is counterproductive, and why a normal scan does not mean "nothing is wrong."

## Concussion Is a Functional Injury, Not a Structural One

### What "Functional" Actually Means

A concussion is a complex pathophysiological process affecting the brain, induced by biomechanical forces. The defining feature for clinical practice is that it is a FUNCTIONAL disturbance of brain physiology rather than a STRUCTURAL lesion. The hardware is intact; the way it processes energy and signals is temporarily deranged.

This is why standard CT and MRI scans are typically normal in concussion. As an EP you will not be ordering imaging, and you do not need to interpret it — but you do need to understand its implications, because patients routinely misread a normal scan as proof that they are fine.

[KEYPOINT: "My scan was clear" does NOT mean "I have recovered." Routine CT and MRI image structure, not function. They are ordered to rule OUT a bleed or fracture (a structural emergency), not to rule IN or OUT a concussion. A patient can have a completely normal scan and a profoundly dysfunctional brain.]

Concussion is classified as a mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI). The word "mild" describes the absence of structural damage and the initial presentation — it does NOT describe the symptom burden, the functional limitation, or the recovery trajectory, all of which can be significant.

### Why This Matters for the EP

Because the injury is functional and metabolic, it is responsive to physiological intervention — and exercise is a physiological intervention. This is the conceptual bridge for the whole course: a structural lesion needs time or surgery to heal, but a dysfunctional, energy-starved, poorly-regulated system can be retrained and re-regulated through carefully dosed exertion.

- Structural injury: damage you can see on a scan (haemorrhage, contusion, fracture). Managed medically/surgically. NOT what you are rehabilitating.

- Functional injury (concussion): physiological and metabolic disturbance, normal scan, symptom-driven. This IS what graded exercise targets.

**The next section opens the "black box" of that functional disturbance — the neurometabolic cascade — and reframes it as an energy crisis, the single most useful concept an EP can hold about concussion.**

## The Neurometabolic Cascade: The Brain's Energy Crisis

At the cellular level, the biomechanical force of a concussion triggers a cascade of ionic, metabolic and neurochemical changes known as the neurometabolic cascade (Giza & Hovda, 2014). For the EP, the most useful single framing of this cascade is that it produces an ENERGY CRISIS: a brain that suddenly needs far more fuel while simultaneously losing the ability to deliver it.

### How the Energy Crisis Develops

The sequence runs as follows. Each step is worth understanding because it maps directly onto the symptoms your patients describe during exertion.

- MECHANICAL DISRUPTION (immediate): biomechanical force stretches neuronal membranes, opening mechanosensitive ion channels and disrupting the axonal cytoskeleton.

- IONIC FLUX (seconds to minutes): potassium floods OUT of neurons and calcium floods IN; glutamate, the main excitatory neurotransmitter, is dumped into the synapse, causing excitotoxicity. This ionic chaos is what produces the immediate confusion and disorientation.

- THE ENERGY CRISIS (minutes to days): to restore normal ion balance, the sodium-potassium ATPase pumps go into overdrive. Restoring those gradients is energetically expensive, so the brain ramps up glucose metabolism dramatically (hyperglycolysis) and burns through ATP. At exactly the moment demand spikes, cerebral blood flow DROPS. The result is a supply-demand mismatch: maximum fuel demand meets reduced fuel delivery. Lactate accumulates from anaerobic metabolism.

- MITOCHONDRIAL DYSFUNCTION & CALCIUM INJURY (hours to days): the calcium that flooded in impairs the mitochondria — the cell's energy factories — so even the capacity to PRODUCE energy is compromised, deepening the crisis.

- IMPAIRED NEUROTRANSMISSION (hours to weeks): disrupted axonal transport and neurotransmitter systems (acetylcholine, dopamine, serotonin) drive the multi-system cognitive, mood and sleep symptoms.

- RECOVERY (days to weeks): ionic balance is gradually restored, cerebral blood flow normalises, and mitochondrial function recovers. Most uncomplicated concussions resolve within 7-28 days; a meaningful minority (often cited as 10-30%) have prolonged recovery.

[KEYPOINT: The clinical headline for an EP: after a concussion the brain is running a severe energy deficit. Cognitive and physical exertion BOTH draw on the same limited metabolic budget. Exceed the budget and you provoke symptoms; stay just under it and you can actually drive recovery. The entire art of concussion exercise rehab is finding and respecting that budget.]

### Why Fatigue Is Physiological, Not Psychological

When a recovering patient says "I felt fine, then after ten minutes everything fell apart," they are describing the energy crisis in real time. Early in a task the metabolic budget covers demand; once the depleted, poorly-perfused system runs out of headroom, symptoms surge. This is a physiological ceiling, not a lack of effort or motivation. Telling such a patient to "push through" worsens the supply-demand mismatch and reliably backfires.

**The energy crisis is only half the picture. The other half is the brain's impaired ability to regulate blood flow and cardiovascular responses during exertion — autonomic dysfunction — which is the direct physiological cause of exercise intolerance.**

## Autonomic Dysfunction & Exercise Intolerance

### The Concept Every Concussion EP Must Own: Exercise Intolerance

Exercise intolerance is the hallmark physiological sign of a physiologically-incompletely-recovered concussion. It is defined as the early, reproducible provocation or worsening of concussion symptoms (typically headache, dizziness, fogginess or visual strain) at a heart rate or workload well below the individual's normal capacity. A previously fit athlete who symptom-limits at a brisk walk is exhibiting exercise intolerance — and that intolerance is measurable, which is exactly why EPs are so well placed to manage it.

The mechanism sits in the autonomic nervous system (ANS) and in cerebral autoregulation — the brain's system for matching its own blood supply to its needs.

### Cerebral Blood-Flow Dysregulation

In a healthy brain, cerebral autoregulation keeps cerebral blood flow remarkably stable across a wide range of blood pressures and activity levels, and tightly couples local flow to local metabolic demand. Concussion disrupts this. Cerebral blood flow is reduced in the acute phase (compounding the energy crisis), and the coupling between neural activity, CO2/blood pressure and cerebral perfusion becomes impaired.

During exercise, this matters acutely. Exertion changes blood pressure, cardiac output and arterial CO2 — all powerful drivers of cerebral blood flow. A brain that can no longer buffer these changes experiences swings in perfusion it cannot regulate, and symptoms are provoked. The patient has not "done damage" by exercising; they have hit the ceiling of a dysregulated control system.

### Impaired Autonomic Control of Heart Rate and Blood Pressure

The same brainstem and central autonomic networks that regulate cerebral blood flow also regulate cardiovascular responses to exertion. After concussion there is frequently a measurable autonomic imbalance — a shift in the sympathetic/parasympathetic balance that shows up as:

- Altered heart rate variability (HRV) — reduced parasympathetic (vagal) tone is commonly reported.

- An exaggerated or blunted heart rate response to a given workload.

- Orthostatic and exertional intolerance, including POTS-like presentations (dizziness, lightheadedness, palpitations on standing or on exertion).

- Symptom provocation at a reproducible heart-rate threshold — the basis of graded exercise testing.

[KEYPOINT: Exercise intolerance after concussion is an AUTONOMIC and CEREBROVASCULAR problem, not a deconditioning or a motivation problem. The symptom-limited heart-rate threshold is reproducible enough to be used as both an assessment tool and a treatment prescription target — which is the entire basis of the protocols you will learn in later modules.]

### Why This Hands the Problem to the EP

Autonomic dysregulation is, in principle, trainable. Sub-threshold aerobic exercise appears to help restore autonomic balance and cerebrovascular regulation — and dosing exertion against a heart-rate threshold, progressing it safely, and monitoring the physiological response is core exercise-physiology practice. The diagnosis of the concussion and the decision that the patient is ready to begin remain with the medical/physiotherapy team; the delivery and progression of the exertion stimulus is the EP's domain.

**Understanding the energy crisis and autonomic dysfunction together explains the single biggest change in concussion care of the last decade — the move away from rest toward active rehabilitation.**

## From Rest to Active Rehab: The Paradigm Shift

### The Old Model: "Rest Until Symptom-Free"

For decades the standard advice was strict physical and cognitive rest until the patient was completely symptom-free — the so-called "cocoon" approach: a dark, quiet room, no screens, no activity. The intuition was reasonable (let the energy-starved brain recover), but taken to an extreme it caused harm.

Prolonged rest is now understood to be counterproductive for most patients. It drives physical deconditioning, worsens the autonomic dysregulation already present, amplifies mood disturbance and anxiety, fosters illness behaviour and fear-avoidance, and is associated with SLOWER, not faster, recovery. Beyond a brief initial period (commonly framed as the first 24-48 hours of relative rest), continued strict rest delays return to normal life.

### The Evidence That Changed Practice

The decisive shift came from controlled trials of sub-symptom-threshold aerobic exercise. Leddy and colleagues (2019, JAMA Pediatrics) randomised adolescents with sport-related concussion, within days of injury, to either individualised sub-symptom-threshold aerobic exercise (prescribed from a graded treadmill test) or to a placebo-like stretching program. The aerobic-exercise group recovered significantly FASTER, and the intervention did not worsen symptoms or cause harm. This and a converging body of work established that early, controlled, sub-threshold exertion is safe and actively therapeutic.

[KEYPOINT: Leddy et al. (2019, JAMA Pediatrics): adolescents prescribed individualised sub-symptom-threshold aerobic exercise early after sport-related concussion recovered faster than those given a stretching/placebo program — with no increase in adverse events. Aerobic exercise moved from "contraindicated" to first-line.]

### What the Current Consensus Says

The international consensus has caught up with the evidence. The Amsterdam consensus statement on concussion in sport (Patricios et al., 2023, the 6th International Conference) endorses early, light, symptom-limited activity and prescribed sub-symptom-threshold aerobic exercise as part of standard management, explicitly moving away from prolonged rest. The principle: be guided by symptoms, keep exertion below the provocation threshold, and progress gradually.

### The New Model in One Sentence

Rest briefly, then move — at a dose that stays just under the symptom threshold, progressed as tolerance improves.

- A short period of relative (not strict) rest in the first day or two.

- Early reintroduction of light activity within symptom limits.

- Individualised, heart-rate-guided sub-symptom-threshold aerobic exercise as an active treatment.

- Graded progression of the exertion stimulus as the symptom-limited threshold rises.

[KEYPOINT: This is exactly why your profession is now in the room. The first-line treatment for concussion is a precisely-dosed, progressively-loaded, objectively-monitored aerobic exercise prescription. That is the definition of what an Accredited Exercise Physiologist does.]

## The Exercise Physiologist in the Concussion Team

### A Clear Division of Labour

Active rehabilitation only works inside a properly bounded multidisciplinary model. Knowing your lane protects the patient and protects you.

- GP / Sports Physician: diagnoses concussion, manages red flags and comorbidities, oversees medical care, and holds ultimate responsibility for return-to-sport clearance.

- Physiotherapist: within scope, contributes to diagnosis and assessment, and treats cervical, vestibular and oculomotor drivers of symptoms.

- Accredited Exercise Physiologist (you): IMPLEMENTS the graded aerobic and exertional rehabilitation — assessing exercise tolerance, prescribing and progressing the program against a symptom-limited threshold, and monitoring physiological and symptom responses.

- The patient/athlete: an active participant who self-reports symptoms honestly, which is the data your dosing depends on.

[KEYPOINT: The EP does NOT diagnose concussion and does NOT clear an athlete for return to sport. You receive a diagnosed patient, deliver and progress the exertion stimulus, document the response, and feed it back to the referring clinician. Clearance decisions belong to medical practitioners.]

### What Falls Inside EP Scope

- Assessing exercise tolerance and identifying the symptom-limited threshold (using validated graded-exertion protocols you will learn later).

- Prescribing individualised sub-symptom-threshold aerobic exercise and progressing it safely.

- Monitoring heart rate, workload, perceived exertion and symptom response, and adjusting dose accordingly.

- Recognising red flags and deterioration, stopping the session, and escalating to the medical team.

- Clear, documented communication with the referring practitioner.

### What Falls Outside EP Scope

- Making or changing the concussion diagnosis.

- Determining medical readiness to commence, or signing off return-to-play clearance.

- Independently managing red-flag or deteriorating presentations beyond stop-and-refer.

Hold this boundary in mind for the rest of the course. Everything you will be taught — graded exertion testing, threshold-based prescription, progression rules, monitoring and stop criteria — sits inside the implementer role. The physiology in this module is the justification for that role: a functional, energy-starved, autonomically-dysregulated injury that responds to precisely-dosed exercise is, by its nature, an exercise physiology problem.
