# Graded Return to Activity, Sport & Performance

---

> Staged Progression, Australian Guidelines and Sport-Specific Reconditioning

Where Module 4 built aerobic tolerance, this module builds the athlete back to their sport. It covers the international graded return-to-sport strategy, the mandatory stand-down requirements of the AIS/SMA Australian Concussion Guidelines for Youth and Community Sport, and the sport-specific reconditioning progressions an Accredited Exercise Physiologist designs and delivers — return-to-running, multidirectional and contact-preparation conditioning, load monitoring, and periodisation. Throughout, it positions the AEP as the reconditioning specialist who progresses a symptomatic athlete to a well-prepared one and hands them — with documented evidence of readiness — to the clinician who makes the final return-to-contact decision. The AEP delivers and recommends; the AEP does not diagnose and does not clear.

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this module you will be able to:

- Apply the international graded return-to-sport strategy as a staged, symptom-monitored progression you deliver as an AEP.

- Comply with the AIS/SMA Australian Concussion Guidelines for Youth and Community Sport stand-down requirements and locate the AEP's role correctly within them.

- Design sport-specific reconditioning progressions — including a return-to-running progression and team-sport conditioning from linear through multidirectional to contact-preparation.

- Define objective progress, regress and readiness criteria tied to symptom response and load tolerance.

- Communicate an athlete's reconditioning status and readiness to the clinician responsible for the final return-to-contact decision.

[KEYPOINT: Scope of practice for AEPs: You deliver, monitor and progress the graded reconditioning. You do NOT diagnose concussion and you do NOT provide medical clearance for return to contact or competition — that is a medical decision. Your job is to take the athlete as far as the highest non-contact, non-cleared stage allows, document that they tolerate full sport-specific load symptom-free, and hand a well-prepared athlete to the clearing clinician with a clear recommendation.]

## The AEP's Role: Reconditioning Specialist, Not Gatekeeper

Concussion return-to-sport is a relay, not a solo event. The diagnosing and clearing roles are medical. The reconditioning role — the substantial, skilled, day-to-day work of rebuilding an athlete's capacity to run, change direction, accelerate, decelerate, absorb load and execute sport skills without symptom provocation — is exercise-physiology territory. This module is about owning that role precisely.

Think of the graded return-to-sport strategy as a ladder. The AEP delivers the rungs. There is one rung near the top — the transition into full-contact training and competitive contact — that has a locked gate, and only a medical practitioner holds the key. The AEP's craft is to bring the athlete all the way up to that gate in the best possible condition, with documented evidence that they tolerate full sport-specific, non-contact load, so the clearing clinician is making a decision about a prepared athlete rather than a deconditioned one.

### What the AEP owns

- Designing and progressing the symptom-limited aerobic, strength and sport-specific reconditioning program.

- Monitoring symptom response within and between sessions and adjusting load accordingly.

- Building sport-specific capacities: running mechanics and volume, multidirectional movement, acceleration/deceleration, reactive agility, and conditioning to the demands of the athlete's sport.

- Documenting tolerance, load and symptom data and communicating readiness to the clearing clinician.

### What the AEP does NOT own

- The concussion diagnosis.

- Progression INTO full-contact training or competitive contact — this requires medical clearance.

- The final return-to-sport (contact) decision and any associated clearance documentation.

**The strongest professional position an AEP can hold is "I will bring this athlete to the door in peak condition and tell you exactly what I observed — you decide when the door opens." That is both clinically correct and scope-compliant.**

## The Graded Return-to-Sport Strategy (Amsterdam 2023)

The internationally recognised graded return-to-sport (GRTS) strategy, restated in the 2023 Amsterdam Consensus Statement on Concussion in Sport, is a six-stage progression. It is the framework every Australian return-to-sport pathway is built on. The AEP delivers stages 1 through 4 and, after medical clearance, supports stages 5 and 6.

[KEYPOINT: Core rule: a minimum of 24 hours is spent at each stage, and the athlete must tolerate the stage without new or worsening concussion symptoms before progressing. If symptoms recur, drop back to the previous tolerated stage. Medical clearance is required BEFORE stage 5 (full-contact practice). The athlete should be back to their normal academic or work activity before resuming contact.]

### The six stages

- Stage 1 — Symptom-limited activity: daily activities that do not provoke symptoms; gradual reintroduction of routine. Light cognitive and physical activity as tolerated. (AEP/medical co-managed.)

- Stage 2 — Light aerobic exercise: walking or stationary cycling at light-to-moderate intensity, sub-symptom-threshold, to increase heart rate. No resistance training. (This is the FITT-based sub-symptom-threshold aerobic work from Module 4 — squarely AEP-delivered.)

- Stage 3 — Sport-specific exercise: running or skating drills, individual sport-specific movement; no head-impact activity. Adds movement, change of pace and coordination. (AEP-delivered — the core of this module.)

- Stage 4 — Non-contact training drills: harder training drills (e.g. passing drills), progressive resistance training, more complex movement and cognitive load. The athlete may resume the full non-contact training menu. (AEP-delivered.)

- Stage 5 — Full-contact practice: following medical clearance, normal training activities including body contact. Restores confidence and lets coaching staff assess functional skills. (Requires medical clearance first; AEP may support conditioning.)

- Stage 6 — Return to sport: normal competitive game play.

The early stages (1–3) are typically progressed at minimum 24-hour intervals, but the calendar minimums in the Australian guidelines (next section) override any temptation to rush — particularly in youth and community sport, where the stand-down is a hard floor regardless of how quickly symptoms settle.

**The six stages tell you the SEQUENCE. The Australian guidelines tell you the MINIMUM TIME. The next section reconciles the two.**

## Australian Compliance: The AIS/SMA Stand-Down Requirements

In Australia, the AIS/SMA Australian Concussion Guidelines for Youth and Community Sport set conservative, mandatory minimum timeframes for youth and community-level athletes. These are more protective than the bare 24-hour-per-stage international minimums, and as an AEP working with community and youth athletes you must program inside them.

[KEYPOINT: Two non-negotiable minimums for youth and community sport: (1) a MINIMUM 21-day stand-down from the time of the concussion before return to competitive contact/collision activity; and (2) the athlete must be symptom-free for at least 14 days BEFORE returning to contact training. Light exercise and non-contact, sport-specific reconditioning can begin earlier (sub-symptom-threshold), but the contact gate cannot open before these minimums are met — and only on medical clearance.]

Practically, this means the symptom-free clock and the calendar clock run together, and the later of the two governs. An athlete who becomes symptom-free quickly still cannot return to competitive contact before day 21. An athlete who reaches day 21 but has only been symptom-free for 6 days still cannot enter contact training, because the 14-day symptom-free requirement for contact training has not been satisfied.

### How this reshapes the GRTS ladder for the AEP

- You can and should begin sub-symptom-threshold aerobic and non-contact sport-specific reconditioning (GRTS stages 2–4) within the stand-down window, provided each stage is tolerated symptom-free. The stand-down is not a "do nothing" period — it is a "no contact" period.

- You pace the non-contact reconditioning so the athlete arrives at full non-contact training capacity (top of stage 4) at or before the point where the calendar and symptom-free minimums are satisfied — not weeks of deconditioning followed by a rushed re-entry.

- You do NOT progress the athlete into contact training (the start of stage 5 territory) until BOTH the ≥14-day symptom-free requirement and the medical clearance are in place. That progression and clearance are the clinician's call.

[KEYPOINT: Do not put a date stamp on these guidelines in your patient communications — refer to them simply as the AIS/SMA Australian Concussion Guidelines for Youth and Community Sport. They represent the current Australian community-sport standard. Elite and professional pathways may run under sport-specific protocols with team medical oversight; when in doubt, the conservative community-sport minimums are the safe default.]

**Compliance summary: sequence by Amsterdam 2023, time-gate by AIS/SMA, clearance by the medical practitioner, reconditioning by you.**

## Designing a Return-to-Running Progression

Running is the foundational re-loading task for almost every field, court and endurance athlete, and progressing it is core AEP work. A concussion-specific return-to-running progression differs from a standard tissue-loading return-to-run in one critical way: the limiting signal is symptom response (and autonomic tolerance), not musculoskeletal pain. You progress on the basis of "no new or worsening concussion symptoms during or in the 24 hours after the session", layered on top of normal load-management principles.

This progression sits inside GRTS stage 3 (sport-specific exercise) and feeds stage 4. It assumes the athlete has already established sub-symptom-threshold aerobic tolerance (Module 4) and is symptom-free or stable at light aerobic load.

### A staged return-to-running progression

- Step 1 — Walk/jog intervals: alternating walk and easy jog blocks (e.g. 2 min walk / 1 min jog), keeping heart rate sub-symptom-threshold. Flat, predictable surface. Goal: reintroduce impact and vertical head movement at low dose.

- Step 2 — Continuous easy running: progress to continuous jogging at conversational pace within the HR ceiling, building duration before pace. Goal: sustained linear running tolerated symptom-free.

- Step 3 — Tempo / sustained moderate running: lift the intensity toward steady tempo efforts within tolerance, introducing a higher but still controlled heart-rate and breathing load. Goal: demonstrate the athlete tolerates a genuine cardiovascular training stimulus.

- Step 4 — Interval / repeated-effort running: structured intervals (e.g. repeated 200–400 m efforts with recovery) reintroducing acceleration, higher heart rates and the repeated-effort profile of sport. Goal: tolerate intensity spikes and the autonomic swings they produce.

- Step 5 — Change-of-direction running: introduce curved running, gentle cutting, decelerations and re-accelerations, and head/eye movement under running load. Goal: add the vestibular-ocular and multidirectional demand that pure linear running omits.

- Step 6 — Sport-specific running drills: integrate sport-shaped running — shuttle patterns, position-specific movement, reactive cues, ball/implement work — at training intensity. Goal: full non-contact running capacity, ready to feed into team training.

[KEYPOINT: Progress one variable at a time. Increase duration before pace, pace before intensity, and linear load before multidirectional load. Stacking volume, intensity and change-of-direction in the same step is the fastest way to provoke symptoms and lose the thread on what caused them.]

Change-of-direction and reactive running are where covert vestibular-ocular intolerance often surfaces — an athlete who is fine running in a straight line can become symptomatic when rapid head turns and gaze shifts are added. Treat the appearance of symptoms at step 5 as useful diagnostic information to feed back to the referrer, not simply a programming failure.

## Team-Sport Conditioning: Linear to Multidirectional to Contact-Prep

For collision and team-sport athletes, return-to-running is necessary but not sufficient. The AEP rebuilds the specific physical qualities the sport demands — repeated high-speed efforts, accelerations and decelerations, multidirectional agility, and the physical robustness to prepare for (but not yet enter) contact. This is a layered conditioning progression delivered across GRTS stages 3 and 4, completed before the athlete is handed to the clinician for the contact decision.

### Phase A — Linear conditioning

- Re-establish straight-line running volume and repeated-effort capacity (built via the return-to-running progression above).

- Reintroduce progressive resistance training, starting light and building, to rebuild strength lost during the symptomatic/rest period. Avoid heavy Valsalva straining early; progress as symptoms allow.

- Rebuild aerobic and repeated-sprint conditioning to a baseline that supports the multidirectional work to come.

### Phase B — Multidirectional conditioning

- Layer in pre-planned change-of-direction drills, agility ladders, shuttle work and acceleration/deceleration mechanics.

- Progress from pre-planned (closed) to reactive (open) agility — responding to a coach's cue, a ball, or an opponent's movement — which reintroduces the cognitive-motor and vestibular-ocular dual-task load of real play.

- Integrate sport skills (passing, catching, kicking, dribbling) under movement and fatigue, mirroring training demands.

### Phase C — Contact-preparation conditioning

- Build the physical robustness that precedes contact: bracing, falling and getting up, wrestling/grappling drills with a cooperative partner, tackle-bag and shield work, and controlled collision-preparation drills that do NOT involve head impact or competitive contact.

- This conditions the body for the demands of contact while staying within the non-contact boundary the AEP must respect.

[KEYPOINT: Contact-PREPARATION (bag work, controlled bracing, non-head-impact partner drills) is AEP-deliverable reconditioning. Actual full-contact PRACTICE (GRTS stage 5) is the gated step requiring medical clearance and satisfaction of the ≥14-day symptom-free and ≥21-day stand-down minimums. The line is head impact and competitive/uncontrolled contact — stay on the preparation side of it until the athlete is cleared.]

At the completion of phase C the athlete should be a fully conditioned, sport-ready athlete in every respect except cleared contact. That is precisely the athlete you hand to the clearing clinician.

## Load Monitoring and Periodisation

The discipline that separates AEP-delivered reconditioning from generic "do some running" advice is structured load monitoring. Because the limiting signal in concussion is symptom response, you monitor both external/internal training load AND symptom load together, and you periodise the build so the athlete arrives sport-ready as the stand-down clock expires — neither under-prepared nor over-cooked.

### Load-monitoring tools

- Session RPE (sRPE): multiply the session's rating of perceived exertion (Borg 0–10 or 6–20) by session duration in minutes to get a single internal-load number (arbitrary units). Simple, validated, and ideal for tracking the build and spotting spikes.

- Heart rate: continue using the heart-rate ceiling and HR response from the aerobic-rehab phase as an objective intensity governor and an autonomic-recovery marker (resting and submaximal HR trends).

- Acute:chronic workload ratio (ACWR): track the relationship between recent (acute) and rolling-average (chronic) load to avoid sharp spikes that elevate both symptom-recurrence and soft-tissue injury risk. Build chronic load progressively rather than ramping acutely.

- Symptom diary: a brief daily symptom rating (and the 24-hour-post-session check) sits alongside the load metrics. The pairing of load and symptom data is what tells you whether the athlete is tolerating the build.

### Periodisation principles

- Work backwards from the earliest compliant return-to-contact date (governed by the ≥21-day stand-down and ≥14-day symptom-free minimums and medical clearance) and plan the non-contact reconditioning to peak there.

- Progress load conservatively and monotonically where possible; avoid large week-to-week jumps in total load.

- Sequence qualities: aerobic base, then linear running volume, then intensity, then multidirectional and reactive load, then contact-preparation — the same logical order used throughout this module.

[KEYPOINT: The 24-hour rule still governs everything: a session is only "tolerated" if it provokes no new or worsening symptoms during the session AND in the following 24 hours. A delayed symptom flare the next day means the prior session was too much — regress and rebuild. Load numbers inform the plan; the symptom response decides it.]

## Progress/Regress Criteria, Readiness and Handover

Every stage transition in this module runs on the same decision logic. Making it explicit keeps your programming defensible and your documentation clean.

### Progress criteria — advance when ALL are met

- The current stage was completed without new or worsening concussion symptoms, during and in the 24 hours after.

- The minimum time at the current stage has elapsed (and any overriding AIS/SMA calendar minimum is respected).

- Objective load and HR responses are stable or improving.

- The athlete reports confidence and readiness to progress.

### Regress criteria — drop back when ANY occur

- New or worsening concussion symptoms during or after the session (including a delayed next-day flare).

- A meaningful, unexplained rise in submaximal heart rate or fall in tolerance.

- Loss of confidence, fear or anxiety about the task.

- Any red flag that warrants pausing and referring back to the diagnosing/medical clinician.

When regressing, return to the last fully tolerated stage, allow recovery, and rebuild — usually progressing more smoothly the second time because the offending variable is now known.

### Readiness for handover (the AEP's endpoint)

- The athlete tolerates full non-contact, sport-specific training load (top of GRTS stage 4) symptom-free, during and after.

- They have completed the return-to-running progression through sport-specific drills and the team-sport conditioning through contact-preparation.

- Load, HR and symptom records show a stable, symptom-free build at full non-contact intensity.

- They have returned to their normal academic or work activity.

[KEYPOINT: At this point the AEP's role is to RECOMMEND, not to clear. Reaching readiness does not authorise contact — it qualifies the athlete to be assessed for clearance by the medical practitioner, who also verifies the ≥21-day stand-down and ≥14-day symptom-free minimums are satisfied.]

### Communicating readiness to the clearing clinician

- Provide a concise written summary: stages completed, dates, the load/symptom trajectory, and any events (e.g. symptoms that appeared with change-of-direction at step 5 and subsequently resolved).

- State plainly what the athlete now tolerates: "tolerates full non-contact sport-specific training and contact-preparation conditioning, symptom-free, during and at 24 hours."

- Make a clear, scope-appropriate recommendation: "from a reconditioning standpoint the athlete is prepared for the contact-clearance assessment" — and explicitly leave the clearance decision and the contact progression to the clinician.

**A clean handover note does two things at once: it gives the clinician real data to clear on, and it documents that you stayed inside your scope. Both protect the athlete and protect you.**
